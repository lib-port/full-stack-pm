# Chapter 16: Adoption, Distribution, and Growth

## An account is not a working day

A plumbing business has opened a Cedar account, but its dispatcher still prepares tomorrow's visits in the old spreadsheet. The new workspace lacks the customer addresses and open jobs she needs. The owner has also asked the company's accountant to approve the connection to its accounting system. Until those tasks are resolved, using Cedar would mean maintaining two sets of records and working out separately which jobs are ready to invoice.

The owner knows about the product. The dispatcher has seen a demonstration. Neither needs another announcement that Cedar exists. They need a workable transition from the arrangement that currently keeps the business running.

In this stage of the case, Cedar discovers that successful onboarding depends strongly on importing customer and job data and connecting accounting systems. The limiting factor is switching cost, not awareness. That finding changes the next product decision: improve the transition, establish what help customers need and check whether they can begin useful work. Increasing promotional activity would bring more people towards the same obstruction.

**Adoption** means taking an offering into use. For an operational product, it involves people, information, permissions and working practices. Opening an account is an observable event, but it is not equivalent to a repair business relying on the product during a busy day.

**Onboarding** is the process of helping someone become able to use the product in their circumstances. It can include setup, learning, importing information, changing responsibilities and completing initial work. A guided tour of the interface is one possible component. It cannot replace the records or authority needed to act.

**Activation** is a defined early event that indicates someone has begun experiencing the product's intended usefulness. The definition must fit the product and be checked against subsequent experience. For Cedar, a candidate might involve a dispatcher organising real visits from usable records, technicians receiving the necessary information and the business completing the agreed accounting handoff. This is a proposed indicator, not proof of lasting benefit.

A narrower activation event may be easier to measure. “Created one appointment” is simple, but a demonstration appointment could satisfy it while the business remains unable to operate. Choose an event that provides useful evidence without pretending one event captures the entire outcome. Record what the event establishes and what it leaves unknown.

This changes how a product manager sees growth. Marketing can help suitable customers discover the offer; sales can help them assess it; design, engineering, support and customer staff must make useful adoption possible. The boundaries between those responsibilities matter less than whether the person can complete the next necessary action.

Learning also continues after the first successful task. The dispatcher may understand how to add a straightforward visit while still needing help with a cancellation or a technician's absence. Onboarding should prepare people for the work they will encounter and make later help discoverable. Declaring onboarding complete because someone dismissed every introductory message rewards a different behaviour from being ready to operate.

## Find where adoption stops

**Awareness** means knowing the offering exists. **Acquisition** concerns bringing new customers or users into a defined relationship with it. Be explicit about that relationship: a visitor, trial account and paying customer are different things. A report that calls all three “acquired users” makes it difficult to see what has changed.

**Distribution** concerns how an offering becomes accessible to the intended customer. A distribution channel connects the people and organisations through which the offer reaches them.[^c16-n01] For software, that might involve direct online access, a salesperson, a specialist reseller or a partner who helps a business adopt. For a physical product, availability, delivery and installation can determine whether the customer can obtain the benefit at all.

Map the actions between discovering an offer and obtaining value. For Cedar, an owner may request a demonstration, a dispatcher inspect the workflow, an accountant approve a connection and technicians learn how to obtain assignments. The same business can advance in one activity while being blocked in another. A simple sequence is a useful starting diagram, but it should not conceal parallel work or returns to an earlier decision.

Beside each action, record the actor, the information needed, the reason to proceed and the possible interruption. “Import data” is still too broad. Who can export the old records? Who checks duplicate customers? Who decides which open jobs to carry across? Which information must be available on the first day? These questions expose work that a signup count cannot show.

**Switching friction** is the effort, uncertainty and disruption involved in moving from the current arrangement. Some friction reflects a genuine task, such as checking a customer address. Some comes from avoidable product difficulty, such as an unclear import error. Distinguishing them prevents a team from promising a frictionless transition that simply leaves necessary work for someone else.

Cedar could propose an import preview showing what will be accepted, what requires attention and who must resolve it. It could offer a supported first import or provide a clearer accounting-connection checklist. These are different interventions. Choose among them using evidence about where people stop and why, then check whether the change helps without corrupting records or increasing hidden support work.

Do not treat every unfinished setup as the same failure. One company may be waiting for a planned quiet week; another may have decided the product is unsuitable. A third may have asked for help that Cedar has not provided. Records of screen activity show actions within the product. Conversations and observation may be needed to understand what is happening outside it.

The distinction applies beyond commercial growth. A public-service application may be widely known yet inaccessible to someone who lacks the documents, confidence or assistance needed to complete it. GDS guidance on assisted digital support starts with researching what prevents completion and tailoring support to that need.[^c16-n02] A clearer advertisement cannot supply missing assistance.

Compare people at the same relevant stage. Looking only at customers who completed migration can reveal how success happened, but it omits people who stopped. Looking only at support requests omits people who struggled without asking. Bring those perspectives together before deciding that one obstacle explains every failed adoption. Cedar's stipulated switching constraint directs investigation; it does not remove differences between individual transitions.

## Choose a route that can deliver the change

In a **self-service** approach, customers can assess, obtain and begin using the offering largely through the product and supporting material. In a **sales-assisted** approach, people help prospective customers establish fit, resolve questions and progress towards purchase. Neither label determines how much implementation support will be needed after the purchase.

A simple personal tool may be suitable for self-service because one person controls the decision and can begin with little setup. Cedar's migration involves several people and existing business information. It may need assistance at a particular step even if customers discover the product and open accounts independently. Design around the actual dependency rather than making every customer follow the same sales process.

Human assistance has a cost, but it also provides a way to learn. A support specialist helping a dispatcher prepare an import can identify confusing records and missing instructions. The product manager should preserve that learning, distinguish recurring problems from unusual cases and decide what should become easier within the product. Indefinitely relying on undocumented staff improvisation hides the true delivery model.

A **channel partner** helps the offer reach or serve customers. Consider two potential partners for Cedar. An accountant may already understand a client's invoicing arrangements and be able to help assess a connection. A trade association may reach many suitable businesses and provide a place to explain the coordination problem. These are different contributions, with different evidence requirements.

For the accountant route, investigate whether accountants want this role, what authority clients give them, what competence and access the work requires, and how errors are handled. An introduction does not establish that the accountant will perform migration work. A proposed partnership needs explicit responsibilities and workable incentives for all parties.

For the trade-association route, investigate whether its members fit the target and whether the association can provide useful access. A well-attended presentation could increase awareness while leaving the switching problem untouched. Its value might be stronger if it helps interested members understand the transition and find appropriate implementation support. That remains a proposal to test.

Compare channels using the customers who obtain useful service and the effort required to support them. A route producing many introductions may create substantial qualification work. Another may bring fewer but better-prepared businesses. Include partner fees, staff time, support obligations and the quality of the customer relationship when assessing economics. Cheap contact acquisition is not necessarily cheap customer acquisition.

Keep responsibility visible to the customer. If the accountant, partner and Cedar each assume another party will resolve an import failure, adding a channel has added a gap. Explain whom the customer contacts and how the organisations coordinate a response. Involve engineering and data colleagues in migration reliability, and accounting specialists in the meaning of transferred records. Distribution cannot be separated from delivery when the route makes promises about implementation.

## Earn continued use and further adoption

**Retention** concerns whether a defined group continues a relevant relationship or activity over a stated period. Paying subscription retention, repeated task completion and continued use by a dispatcher answer different questions. Name the population, activity and interval before interpreting a retention figure. A yearly task does not become unsuccessful merely because people do not perform it weekly.

For Cedar, continued use should be examined alongside whether the work remains dependable. Staff might keep using a system because leaving is difficult, even while spending time correcting records elsewhere. Retention is therefore a signal to investigate, not a complete measure of value. A useful product earns continued reliance by fitting the work and handling the exceptions that occur after setup.

Habit can help established behaviour continue. A learned response to a familiar cue might lead a dispatcher to check Cedar when preparing the morning schedule. The design should support that useful routine with clear, dependable information. Repeated notifications intended only to increase visits could interrupt the very work the product is supposed to help. Frequency matters in relation to the task.

**Expansion** occurs when an existing customer increases the extent of a relevant relationship: another office adopts, more staff use the product or the customer buys an additional capability. Genuine expansion can expose new requirements. A second office may have different permissions or accounting arrangements. Counting more accounts before checking those dependencies mistakes administrative growth for successful use.

**Referrals** occur when existing customers direct other people towards the offer. A satisfied owner might recommend Cedar to another business, but the second business still needs to fit and complete its own transition. An incentive for introductions can change whom people refer. Monitor suitability and consequences rather than assuming every introduction expresses independent enthusiasm.

**Diffusion** describes how an offering or practice spreads through a population over time. Relative advantage, compatibility with existing circumstances and difficulty of use are relevant considerations in adoption theory.[^c16-n03] In practice, one business may want evidence from a similar operator, while another can trial a change with little disruption. These are conditions to investigate, not fixed personality types that determine when someone will adopt.

A **growth loop** is a proposed mechanism in which an activity produces something that helps generate further activity. Cedar might help a business complete useful work; the owner then recommends it; a suitable business adopts; that owner may later recommend it. Each link requires a reason and evidence. A customer does not automatically become a promoter, and an introduction does not automatically become adoption.

This differs from a network effect. A referral loop can bring more customers without making Cedar more useful to each existing customer merely because the total has increased. Equally, a product might become more useful through participation without generating many referrals. Separate the mechanisms so that the product manager knows which relationship to investigate.

The time between links matters too. An owner may wait until the business has used Cedar through a busy period before recommending it. A referred business may wait for its next accounting cycle before moving records. Even a working loop can therefore operate slowly. Planning as though recommendations create immediate new revenue ignores both customer decisions and the capacity needed to serve them.

## Test the growth mechanism

Growth has limits. **Saturation** arises when further growth becomes constrained by how much of a relevant population or opportunity is already reached or served. Define that population and period. A channel may repeatedly reach the same suitable businesses while other channels remain unexplored. A product may serve most of a narrow segment but still have an expensive or unsuitable offer for adjacent groups.

Capacity can constrain growth before demand is exhausted. If every new Cedar customer requires a support specialist to clean records, generating more interest can lengthen the wait to begin useful work. The next improvement may involve capacity, clearer data preparation or safer automation. Reducing the queue by accepting incomplete records could merely move the failure into daily operation.

Revisit the constraint after a successful change. Once imports become manageable, permission to connect accounts, staff learning or weak ongoing value may become the next limit. A bottleneck diagnosis belongs to a particular situation and time. Keeping last quarter's answer after circumstances change can make an effective intervention into an expensive routine.

AI can help organise evidence about these interruptions. Supply authorised, appropriately minimised onboarding notes and support records, then ask for a map:

> Group records by the action the customer was trying to complete, the actor involved and the reported interruption. Link every classification to its source. Separate direct statements from inferred explanations. Preserve cases that fit several groups and identify missing information. Suggest checks that would distinguish the leading explanations before proposing growth tactics.

Inspect a sample from each group, including records that contradict the summary. Check whether several tickets describe one customer's continuing problem. The assistant must not treat silence as successful onboarding or count a generated explanation as a reported cause. If someone never contacts support, their difficulty may be absent from the supplied material.

Use the map to compare a specific intervention with the current arrangement. For a supported import, examine completion, record correctness, time to useful work and staff effort. Keep a way to recover from errors. The purpose is to learn whether the intervention relieves the identified constraint, not simply whether people accept an offer of help.

Draw the adoption path for one product you work on. Identify the first point at which a suitable person cannot complete necessary work. State your evidence and one competing explanation. Propose a change, a sign that useful progress occurred and a consequence that would make the change unacceptable. Then ask whether your proposed channel helps people cross that point or merely brings more people to it.

Cedar's growth question now includes records, responsibilities, learning, channels and continued service. The product manager can connect those elements and choose the next action because the obstruction is visible. A campaign can contribute to that system; the campaign alone cannot make the product useful.
