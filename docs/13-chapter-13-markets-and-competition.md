# Chapter 13: Markets and Competition

## The alternative already doing the job

The owner of a small maintenance business decides to keep the existing dispatch arrangement. The dispatcher updates a spreadsheet, messages technicians when plans change and writes urgent reminders on paper. She also remembers which technician knows a troublesome site and which customers need a telephone call. Cedar offers a more integrated arrangement, but the owner has not decided that replacing the current one is worth the effort.

For this customer, Cedar's strongest competition is the combination already in use. A comparison of software vendors could show Cedar leading on several features and still explain little about the owner's decision. The owner is choosing whether to change how the business operates, not merely which application has the longest list.

An **alternative** is an arrangement the customer could choose to address the relevant problem. It may be another product, an internal process, a collection of tools, outside help or continuing as before. What matters is whether it can compete for the customer's choice and resources. An alternative does not need to look like your product to take the investment your product would require.

**Direct competitors** offer broadly similar ways of meeting the same need for the customers being considered. **Indirect competitors** address that need through a different approach. These labels help organise investigation, but their usefulness depends on the decision. Another field-service application may be direct competition for Cedar, while a dispatch service provided by an external office may be indirect competition.

A **substitute** can take the place of an offering for some purpose. The dispatcher's spreadsheet is a substitute for some scheduling functions, though it does not independently replace every part of Cedar. A **complement** makes another offering more useful when used with it. An accounting connection can complement Cedar by helping job information enter the financial workflow. One organisation can supply a complement in one context and compete in another.

The question defining the choice matters. “Which scheduling application should we buy?” produces a different set of alternatives from “How should we coordinate next year's repair work?” The second could include hiring another coordinator, changing the service area or improving current procedures. Broadening the question is useful when the narrower question has already assumed the solution.

For product analysis, treat the **market** as the field of relevant customers, needs and alternatives you are examining. It is not simply a category printed in an industry report. Your positioning and problem definition affect which alternatives matter, but changing your preferred category does not change what customers can actually choose. Formal competition-law analysis has its own purposes and requires appropriate expertise; a product team's practical map serves a different decision.

Start with the arrangement doing the work today. Ask what it accomplishes, why people retain it and what would have to change for them to choose differently. The answer gives feature comparison a purpose and a boundary.

The relevant resource may also sit outside the apparent product category. A small business might choose between buying software and adding a part-time office employee. Those options do not need identical functions to compete for a limited operating budget. This does not make every purchase in the organisation a direct competitor. It means the product manager should investigate the actual decision and distinguish a practical budget alternative from a remote theoretical possibility. The owner's next best option matters more than an exhaustive list of things money could buy.

## The cost of leaving

The maintenance company's current arrangement has weaknesses, but replacing it requires work. Customer records must move out of spreadsheets. Technicians must learn where to find updates. The dispatcher must translate knowledge held in memory into something others can use. During the transition, the business still needs to organise tomorrow's visits.

These are **switching costs**: the money, effort, time and risk associated with moving from one arrangement to another. They can arise even when there is no cancellation fee and the new product is cheaper. Familiarity, accumulated information, connected systems and working relationships can all make change consequential.

Be precise about the burden. “Customers resist change” does not tell Cedar what to improve. “The dispatcher must reconcile duplicate customer records before the new schedule is reliable” identifies work that could influence the decision. “The owner cannot risk disrupting dispatch during the busiest month” identifies a timing constraint. These explanations permit different responses.

Switching can also redistribute authority. The dispatcher who currently resolves exceptions from memory may worry that a shared system will make her knowledge less visible or force her into unsuitable procedures. That is a hypothesis to investigate respectfully, not a claim that she is obstructive. Ask what decisions she needs to retain and how the new arrangement would support them.

A better feature set may therefore be insufficient. Cedar might need an import process, help with initial configuration, a gradual changeover or clearer evidence that operations will continue. Those capabilities affect the competitive offer even when they are absent from a comparison table. The cost of adopting the product belongs beside the value of using it.

Some suppliers deliberately make leaving difficult, while some switching costs follow from useful relationships and accumulated work. Distinguish the two. A product manager should not assume that trapping customer data creates a healthy business advantage. It can damage trust, deter adoption and raise issues requiring legal or contractual assessment. Making records usable and responsibilities clear may make customers more willing to enter the relationship.

Retention also needs interpretation. A customer who remains because the product helps them is different from one who remains because moving is currently too difficult. A renewal alone does not identify which situation applies. The distinction matters when judging product quality and when anticipating how customers might react to an easier alternative.

For Cedar, competitive investigation should therefore include the work of switching as well as the work after switching. If the existing arrangement's strongest advantage is continuity, another scheduling feature may have less effect on customer choice than making the transition dependable.

The company might also keep part of its old arrangement. Technicians could continue using a familiar messaging channel while the dispatcher begins recording appointments in Cedar. That combination can be a sensible transition or a source of conflicting information. It deserves investigation as an actual alternative, rather than classification as a failed all-or-nothing adoption. Identify which record is authoritative, who updates it and how exceptions are handled. The competitive question becomes whether the combined arrangement is preferable to the present one, at this stage of change.

## Why some alternatives are hard to challenge

A **barrier to entry** makes it difficult for a new supplier to compete effectively. It may involve the cost of establishing operations, access to customers or essential resources, required knowledge, regulation or incumbent advantages.[^c13-n01] Having to write some software is not by itself evidence of an insurmountable barrier. Ask what a credible entrant would need and why obtaining it would be difficult.

**Economies of scale** exist when producing or serving more lowers average cost over the relevant range.[^c13-n02] A supplier may spread substantial development work across more customers. That can permit lower prices or more investment, but it does not make every additional customer free. Support complexity, coordination and differing requirements can increase costs. Scale advantages need a cost mechanism, not a large customer-count claim.

**Economies of scope** arise when providing activities together costs less than providing them separately. A company supplying both scheduling and invoicing might share customer records, commercial relationships or support knowledge. That potential saving differs from scale: scope concerns the combination of activities, while scale concerns their volume. Integration work and organisational complexity can consume the expected saving.

A **network effect** concerns value to participants: using a service becomes more or less useful as relevant participation changes. In a repair marketplace, more suitable local providers can make the service more useful to households. More households seeking repairs can make it more useful to providers. These are effects between different participant groups. A shared communication service can instead become more useful because more of the people a user needs to contact are reachable on it.[^c13-n03]

This is different from a supplier spreading development cost across customers. Cedar gaining another unrelated service-business subscriber does not automatically improve scheduling for an existing subscriber. There must be a route by which that additional participation changes the existing user's experience. “We have more data” is also incomplete: explain what the data improves, whether it is suitable and whether the organisation can appropriately use it.

Network effects can be local and conditional. A household needing an electrician nearby does not necessarily benefit from thousands of distant providers. More participants can also create congestion, irrelevant offers or competition that makes the service less attractive to some members. The relevant question is which participants create which value for whom, under what conditions.

| Claimed advantage | Question that checks its mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Scale | Which average cost falls as activity increases? |
| Scope | Which resource or activity is shared more economically? |
| Network effect | How does another participant change an existing participant's value? |
| Switching cost | What must the customer do or risk to leave? |
| Entry barrier | What prevents a credible new supplier from competing? |

Several mechanisms can reinforce one another, but they should not be treated as interchangeable. A product that requires a dense local network needs a different starting strategy from one that mainly needs enough customers to cover a large development bill. The distinction changes which evidence the product manager should seek before promising a defensible advantage.

Complements can change those advantages. If several products work with the same accounting system, access to that connection may be an expected condition of competing. If a new integration makes a previously impractical alternative usable, the customer gains another choice without either scheduling product changing its main screen. Track the surrounding arrangements as well as the products whose names appear in a comparison. Dependencies can strengthen an incumbent or open a route for another supplier.

## A moving competitive field

Later, as Cedar pursues larger service businesses, the competing arrangements change. A regional company coordinating several offices may already use specialised operations software, established accounting connections and internal reports. Its alternative might be a more extensive commercial system or extending a system it has built itself. The spreadsheet-and-memory arrangement that dominated the earlier small-business choice is no longer the whole competitive story.

This does not mean every larger organisation has the same requirements. It means Cedar must revisit the choice being made. The buyer may now assess permissions, cross-office visibility, implementation support or compatibility with existing processes. A feature that distinguished Cedar for a small company may be an expected minimum in this different comparison.

**Commoditisation** occurs when buyers come to regard competing offerings or capabilities as sufficiently interchangeable that distinctions carry less weight. A calendar view could become expected across the available products. Adding a similar calendar would then help Cedar meet a minimum requirement but might provide little reason to switch. Price, reliability, service, distribution or fit with a particular workflow could become more influential.

The response is not necessarily to invent a more elaborate feature. Cedar needs to understand where meaningful differences remain. A dependable import process could matter more than another visual option. A simple product may be attractive to a group overwhelmed by extensive systems. Competitive value depends on the customer's circumstances, not the novelty of the implementation.

**Market structure** describes relevant patterns among suppliers and customers: how many credible alternatives exist, how differentiated they are, how easy entry is and how much bargaining power different parties possess. A market with many similar suppliers differs from one dominated by a few well-established providers. Those patterns affect choices, but counting logos is insufficient. Several brands may offer weak alternatives for the particular customer under consideration.

The UK Competition and Markets Authority's guidance similarly treats competitive alternatives and their closeness as matters for evidence, rather than assuming that a category boundary by itself settles competitive strength.[^c13-n04] Product managers can apply that limited insight while leaving formal legal assessments to specialists.

Competitors also respond. If Cedar makes importing information easier, another supplier could improve migration assistance. If Cedar lowers a price, a rival might match it, bundle another service or concentrate on a different group. These are scenarios to assess, not predictions of what a named company will do.

Ask what an alternative's provider can change, what it has reason to defend and how quickly a response could affect the customer. Existing processes can respond too: the dispatcher may improve her spreadsheet, or the owner may hire office help. A static comparison assumes that other people stop adapting while Cedar acts. A useful market view keeps their available choices visible.

## Build a map you can verify

AI can broaden an initial list of competitors, substitutes and complements. The risk is receiving a plausible catalogue that combines outdated products, invented features and irrelevant market categories. Give the assistant a customer situation and a decision before asking for names.

> A small repair business coordinates visits using a dispatcher, spreadsheets, messaging and paper. We are investigating alternatives for dependable coordination. Map direct products, substitute arrangements and complements. Explain what each alternative replaces and what switching would require. Separate verified current facts from candidates to investigate. Do not infer that a product offers a capability merely because its category usually does.

The initial output is a research map. For each candidate that could affect the decision, verify the current product exists and is offered to the relevant customer. Inspect its own documentation for capabilities and limitations. Check current pricing, including the unit charged, minimum commitments and necessary add-ons. Treat company statements about performance as claims requiring appropriate evidence, rather than independent validation.

Record the source, date and unresolved question beside each entry. A vendor's pricing page supports what is publicly advertised on that date; it may not establish negotiated terms or the total adoption cost. A feature description supports the stated capability, not whether it works well in the customer's circumstances. A customer discussion can explain a decision, but one account does not establish every buyer's preference.

Use a small set of decision-relevant comparisons. For Cedar's early customer, useful columns might be daily coordination, handling changes, information continuity and transition effort. For a larger organisation, they might include cross-office operation and integration responsibilities. Let the customer problem determine the columns instead of collecting every feature that is easy to find.

Then test the map against behaviour. Ask which alternatives were seriously considered, what was rejected and why, and what the customer would do if no new purchase were made. The answer may reveal that the most influential alternative was omitted because it did not have a product website.

For your own product, name the strongest current arrangement, one substitute and one complement. Explain the switching work, identify a claimed competitive advantage and test its mechanism using the table above. Finally, write one plausible response from another supplier or from the customer operating the existing process. Mark each statement as evidence, interpretation or open question.

You have a useful competitive view when it explains a customer choice and identifies what could change that choice. A list of similar products is one input. The judgement comes from understanding the alternatives people can actually adopt, retain and improve.

Revisit that view when the target customer, purchase trigger or surrounding technology changes. An old competitor map can remain internally tidy while answering a question the business no longer faces. Keep the map's date and decision visible so another product manager can tell what the evidence supports.
