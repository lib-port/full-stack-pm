# Chapter 14: Business Models and Pricing

## The bill changes the product

The owner of a seasonal maintenance business disables technician accounts before the next billing period. She will enable several of them again when temporary staff return. Cedar's per-active-technician charge has made account administration part of managing the season. The owner wants reliable job records, but she also wants to avoid paying the same account charge for staff with very different levels of work.

In this pricing arrangement, an account enabled during the month attracts the monthly technician charge. A temporary worker needed for a few days can therefore add the same charge as a permanent technician working throughout the month. The owner responds by repeatedly disabling accounts and restoring access when needed. Staff then have to resolve access problems before using the records required for their work.

The rule is understandable as a way to charge larger businesses more. Yet the unit Cedar charges for does not fit this customer's seasonal pattern well. Sharing an account or delaying account creation are further possible responses to investigate. Both could undermine the individual access and clear records that the product is intended to support.

Pricing has therefore changed the product experience. It influences who can use Cedar, how records are created, what office work is necessary and when the customer considers leaving. Deciding the amount of the charge without examining its unit would miss much of the problem.

A **business model** describes how an organisation creates and delivers value and obtains the resources needed to sustain that activity. The **revenue model** is the narrower account of who pays, for what and on what terms. The **cost structure** describes what delivery consumes and how those costs behave. A useful feature, a payment arrangement and a sustainable organisation are related, but they are not the same thing.

**Value capture** means obtaining a share of the economic benefit in a form that supports the organisation. Cedar can help customers coordinate work without necessarily collecting enough revenue to operate dependably. It can also charge customers while making adoption unnecessarily difficult. The design question connects benefit, willingness to exchange, delivery cost and the behaviour induced by the terms.

Start by drawing three flows in words: what each participant receives, what each must do and where money moves. Include operations, support and partners. If one flow depends on work or funding that nobody has agreed to provide, the business model is incomplete even if its revenue forecast looks attractive.

Also distinguish the buyer's decision from the user's task. The owner may evaluate the annual bill while a temporary technician needs access for tomorrow's visit. If saving a small charge prevents the technician retrieving the correct information, the two decisions interact. The product manager should bring those perspectives together: what would make the owner comfortable providing the access that the work requires? That question is more useful than assuming either the owner is unreasonable or the price simply needs to be lower.

## How money reaches the offering

Different revenue models place different demands on the product. Choosing one requires considering the work and relationships it supports, rather than treating recurring revenue or any other form as inherently better.

| Model | What triggers payment | Product question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subscription | Access or service for an agreed period | What continuing benefit and obligation justify renewal? |
| Transaction | A completed exchange or event | Can the event and each party's responsibility be identified reliably? |
| Usage | A measured quantity consumed | Can customers predict and control the bill? |
| Licensing | Permission to use specified rights or capabilities | What is permitted, for how long, and with what support? |
| Advertising | Access to an audience or an advertising result | How does serving advertisers affect the people supplying attention? |

These forms overlap. A licence can be paid periodically. A subscription can include some usage and charge for more. Cedar's starting subscription with usage-dependent charges is one possible combination. The terms should make the combination understandable to the customer and operable by the supplier.

A transaction charge can align revenue with activity that users value, but the transaction must be defined. Is payment due when a visit is booked, completed or paid? Cancellations, refunds and failed payments may create different obligations. A product manager should identify those cases with finance and operations before treating the model as a percentage applied to a clean total.

Usage charging can connect revenue to variable supplier costs, such as message delivery. It can also make customers hesitate before using a beneficial capability. A predictable subscription can reduce that hesitation while exposing the supplier to heavier use. Neither resolves the balance automatically; the product needs terms, visibility and cost management appropriate to its users.

In a marketplace, the operator must consider at least two participant groups. Households need suitable repair providers; providers need worthwhile customer demand. A fee acceptable to one side may discourage the participation needed by the other. The operator may charge providers while letting households search without paying, or use another arrangement that supports both sides' reasons to participate.

This is **cross-subsidy** when revenue from one activity or group helps fund another. The funded activity still has costs. Calling household access free describes the household's payment, not the economics of operating search, resolving disputes or maintaining accurate listings. The operator needs to understand who funds those obligations and whether that funding remains credible as participation changes.

Keep the value of marketplace transactions separate from the operator's revenue. If a household pays a provider £200 and the platform retains £20 under the assumed arrangement, £200 of work has passed through the platform, but only £20 is retained before the platform's own costs. The rest is not available to pay its staff or fund another service.

Payment timing is another design choice. An annual advance payment can provide cash before a year of service has been delivered, while monthly payment spreads the customer's commitment. Cash received does not mean the supplier's future work has disappeared. Refunds, service obligations and recognition in the accounts need an agreed treatment. Compare the buyer's flexibility, the supplier's funding needs and the operational consequences, with finance establishing how those flows should be represented.

## Decide what belongs in the offer

Packaging determines which capabilities and service levels are sold together. **Bundling** combines several elements in one offer. It can simplify buying and support a complete workflow, but it can also make customers pay for things they do not need or obscure the price of an important component.

Cedar could include scheduling and basic invoicing in one subscription while offering specialised reporting separately. That proposal needs a reason grounded in use and economics. If an essential step in everyday coordination requires a surprising extra purchase, the package can frustrate the people the core offer is meant to serve. If expensive specialist work is included for everyone, customers with simple needs may subsidise a service they never use.

**Freemium** provides continuing access to a free version while charging for a paid version or additional capabilities. It differs from a time-limited trial. A free version can help people learn whether a product is useful, but its users still require resources. The model needs a credible account of who pays and what causes suitable users to choose paid access.

That account should not rely on making the free experience needlessly obstructive. For an internal collaboration tool, restricting every useful collaboration action could prevent people discovering its benefit. For an expensive service, unlimited free usage could consume resources faster than paid relationships develop. The product manager needs evidence about use, cost and conversion, not a general assertion that a large free audience will eventually pay.

**Price discrimination** means charging different prices to different customers or circumstances for an otherwise comparable offering, beyond differences explained simply by delivery cost.[^c14-n01] A reduced price for an eligible educational organisation is an accessible example. Different versions, commitments or purchase conditions can also allow customers to choose among prices, although genuine product and cost differences must be distinguished from price differences alone.

The term describes an economic arrangement, not a judgement that every such practice is lawful or fair. Eligibility, transparency, customer expectations and relevant rules require consideration. A product manager should involve appropriate legal and commercial specialists when actual pricing treats groups differently. This introductory concept does not settle those questions.

Packaging and discounts can also create contradictions. A plan intended for smaller firms might remove the very controls a small firm needs to maintain accurate records. A discount designed to encourage longer commitment might lock a seasonal business into unused capacity. Test the offer against a complete customer situation, including who authorises purchase and who does the work after it.

## Choose a unit customers can live with

A **pricing metric** is the unit or basis used to determine a charge: technician accounts, visits, messages, locations or another measure. A **value metric** describes a quantity associated with the benefit the customer obtains. They may align, but they need not be identical. Recorded activity can be easy to charge for while being only loosely related to useful outcomes.

For Cedar's seasonal customer, per-active-technician pricing needs reconsideration. The following alternatives are proposals, each with consequences to investigate:

| Architecture | Potential fit | Behaviour or cost to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base subscription plus technician bands | A predictable range before the next price step | Customers may hesitate to cross a band boundary. |
| Base subscription plus completed visits | Charges rise with recorded service activity | Customers may avoid recording some work or dispute what counts as completed. |
| Annual allowance with seasonal flexibility | Capacity can move between busy and quiet periods | Estimation and reconciliation become part of buying. |
| Charge per operating location | A simple unit for some regional businesses | Locations can differ greatly in activity and support needs. |

The existing per-technician model may still suit some customers. The question is whether its advantages justify the consequences for the intended customer group and whether the definition of active can be improved. Daily proration, a minimum commitment or different seasonal terms each shifts complexity and risk. A product manager should examine the whole arrangement before selecting one appealing adjustment.

Compare each candidate along five connected dimensions. Does the charge grow when the customer receives more value? Can the buyer estimate and explain the bill? Does revenue respond to the supplier's important costs? What action does the unit encourage or discourage? Can the organisation implement, explain and support the rule reliably?

For example, charging per recorded visit could appear aligned with activity. Yet if customers have a reason to omit visits, the pricing rule could damage scheduling and job histories. A location charge could be predictable for the buyer while leaving Cedar exposed to very different workloads. An annual allowance could accommodate seasonality while requiring money or commitment earlier than the customer can comfortably provide.

The metric also affects growth incentives. A charge for each additional colleague makes collaboration more expensive at the moment a customer invites someone. A usage allowance might encourage experimentation until the customer reaches its boundary, then create a sudden bill concern. These are hypotheses about behaviour, which actual customers and operating data must help assess.

Make billing visible enough to reason about. Customers need to understand which activity counts, see what they are using and know how changes affect the next bill. The organisation needs a way to explain corrections and handle disputes. These are product and service capabilities, not merely entries in a price sheet.

A new metric also needs a transition. Existing customers may have planned staffing or signed commitments under the current terms. Model their likely bills under both arrangements, identify material changes and decide how the organisation will explain and implement them. A mathematically cleaner pricing rule can still fail if customers cannot anticipate its effect. Contractual commitments and notice requirements need appropriate review; the product manager's immediate contribution is a clear account of what would change for whom.

## Check the economics of one relationship

**Unit economics** examines revenue and relevant costs for a defined unit, such as a customer relationship, order or service visit. State the unit, period and cost scope. A profitable-looking average can conceal customers whose support or delivery costs differ substantially.

**Contribution margin** is revenue minus variable costs for the unit or activity being analysed. The amount contributes towards fixed costs and, after those are covered, profit.[^c14-n02] It is not profit by itself. A percentage margin divides that contribution by revenue; the calculation still depends on which costs are included.

Use a simplified customer-month example. Assume revenue of £100 and variable delivery/support costs of £30. Contribution is £70, or 70% of revenue. If variable costs rise to £60 for a demanding customer, contribution falls to £40 even though the price is unchanged. Fixed development, management and other overhead still need funding in both cases.

**Customer acquisition cost**, often shortened to CAC, is the acquisition spending assigned to each acquired customer under a stated method. Suppose a defined campaign and sales effort cost £12,000 and produced 20 new paying customers. The average assigned acquisition cost is £600. Include the relevant people and channel costs, match the customer group and period, and allow for time between spending and purchase. Cost per lead is a different measure.

At £70 contribution per active month, recovering £600 takes about 8.6 active months under constant conditions. That simple payback calculation is £600 divided by £70. It assumes contribution arrives as expected, the customer stays, and no additional costs change the picture. It does not establish that Cedar has enough cash to finance the period before recovery.

**Customer lifetime value**, or LTV, is a forecast of the economic value of the relationship over its duration. Different organisations use different revenue, margin and acquisition-cost conventions. Here, use expected contribution before acquisition cost and fixed overhead, and label that scope. Professional marketing guidance similarly distinguishes revenue-based and contribution-based lifetime calculations.[^c14-n03]

If a simplified forecast assumes 18 active months at £70 contribution, the undiscounted contribution total is £1,260. Subtracting £600 acquisition cost leaves £660 before fixed overhead. If the customer stays only six active months, contribution is £420 and the relationship has not recovered acquisition spending. The difference comes from a retention assumption, not an improvement in the arithmetic.

Real relationships rarely remain that simple. Customers may expand, reduce usage, incur support costs unevenly or leave earlier than expected. Future money also differs from money available now. Use finance expertise for discounted forecasts and important investment commitments. A short observation period does not justify assuming many years of stable customer behaviour.

Inspect groups of customers acquired under similar circumstances, often called cohorts, rather than relying only on one blended average. A costly sales-led customer may have different retention and service needs from a self-service customer. Keep the numerator, denominator and cost definitions consistent when comparing them. Unit economics helps expose the model's conditions; it does not convert forecasts into money the business already has.

An average acquisition cost is also not necessarily the cost of the next customer. Early customers may arrive through the founders' existing contacts, while reaching the next group requires different work. Some acquisition spending builds resources useful across several periods. Record how the calculation assigns those costs and what decision the resulting figure is intended to support. Consistency makes comparison possible; it does not remove the need to understand the activity behind the number.

## Model alternatives without inventing buyers

AI can compare pricing architectures and calculate scenarios from supplied inputs. Give it the present rule, customer patterns and explicit assumptions. Ask it to preserve unknowns:

> Compare per-active-technician, technician-band and seasonal-allowance pricing for the supplied customer scenarios. Show the bill, cost assumptions and behaviours each rule could encourage. Separate customer predictability from supplier contribution. Do not invent willingness to pay, conversion, retention or usage. Identify missing data that could reverse the choice, and keep all forecast values labelled as assumptions.

Check the result using small cases: a quiet month, a brief staffing peak, a large steady customer and a correction to an activity record. Recalculate charges and contribution. Ask whether a customer can reduce the bill by acting against the product's purpose. Verify that the model has not treated all recorded transactions as supplier revenue or customer revenue as profit.

Then take two possible pricing units for your own product. Describe one customer whose bill becomes easier to predict under each and one behaviour each unit could discourage. Identify which supplier cost varies with the unit and which does not. Name the evidence needed to choose between them. The exercise succeeds when it reveals a trade-off, not when one option wins every comparison.

Work with finance on cost definitions, cash timing and the interpretation of accounts. Complex accounting, tax, regulated pricing, transfer pricing between related organisations and sophisticated financial models require the relevant professionals. Learning the vocabulary helps you frame the decision; it does not qualify you to determine those requirements alone.

For Cedar, the next step is to investigate seasonal customers' work, billing expectations and account behaviour, then evaluate feasible alternatives with operations and finance. A changed pricing metric must support both the customer's work and the organisation's ability to provide the service. That is why pricing belongs within product judgement from the beginning.
