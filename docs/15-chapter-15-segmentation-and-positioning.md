# Chapter 15: Segmentation and Positioning

## Four businesses behind one label

One business wants fewer fields in the booking form. Another wants clearer control over who can change an appointment. A third wants to compare work across offices. All three requests can sound reasonable when their source is labelled “a field-service customer”. Putting that label on a planning document does not explain which request Cedar should pursue.

Consider four different working arrangements. In a two-person contractor business, the owner also carries out repairs. The two people coordinate directly and need to record an appointment quickly between jobs. Extra approval steps would introduce work without resolving a difficulty established in this situation.

In a ten-technician company, a dedicated dispatcher organises visits while technicians work away from the office. Several changes can require decisions about availability, skills and customer commitments. The dispatcher needs a shared view and a dependable way to get revised information to the people affected. Speed still matters, but coordination has become a separate responsibility.

A fifty-technician regional service company divides work among offices. A local dispatcher may know a particular area well while a regional manager needs to see capacity elsewhere. The organisation has to decide which information is shared and who can reassign work across those boundaries. A single list of every appointment may create visibility without providing useful control.

An enterprise facilities operator coordinates work for multiple client organisations. In this arrangement, procurement, permissions, reporting and connections to client systems affect whether a product can be adopted. The people using a scheduling screen may have little authority to approve a new supplier. A convincing demonstration for a dispatcher is only one part of the purchase.

These are four specific situations, not laws about headcount. A small specialist contractor could have demanding reporting obligations. A larger business could operate through relatively independent local units. Size helps describe the examples, but the working arrangements explain why the choices differ.

**Customer heterogeneity** means customers differ in ways that matter. They may share an industry label while facing different tasks, constraints, alternatives and buying processes. If Cedar attempts to satisfy every request through one undifferentiated offer, it can produce a complicated product that is expensive for the smallest business and inadequate for the most demanding one.

Segmentation makes those differences usable. It groups customers according to characteristics relevant to a decision.[^c15-n01] The purpose is to decide whom to understand, serve and reach, and how the offer should fit. A set of colourful customer profiles is useful only if it changes those decisions in a defensible way.

The first question is therefore practical: which differences would lead Cedar to make different product or commercial choices? The answer begins with work and consequences, then finds descriptions that help the organisation recognise the relevant groups.

Suppose Cedar is deciding whether assignment changes need an approval step. The two-person arrangement gives little reason for one. The regional arrangement creates a question about authority between offices. Grouping those requests by company name or subscription tier would record who asked; grouping them by how assignment authority works could explain the difference. Segmentation is valuable here because it changes the design question from “How many customers requested approval?” to “Which arrangements require approval, by whom, and for what?”

## Choose variables that explain a decision

A **segmentation variable** is a characteristic used to distinguish groups. Geography, company size, work type, frequency of schedule changes, current tools and buying authority could all be candidates for Cedar. Selecting a variable means proposing that it helps explain a difference important enough to act on.

**Demographic** variables describe people, such as age or household circumstances. **Firmographic** variables describe organisations, such as industry, size or location.[^c15-n02] They can be useful for finding or describing prospective customers. They can also be convenient labels that conceal the behaviour the product manager actually needs to understand.

“Companies with ten to fifty technicians” is easy to put into a recruitment brief. It does not establish whether those businesses frequently change appointments, whether they employ a dispatcher or whether their existing arrangements are causing difficulty. Treat company size as a clue to investigate, rather than a substitute for those questions.

**Behavioural segmentation** groups people or organisations by relevant actions or patterns of use. For Cedar, that might mean businesses repeatedly rearranging visits during the day, or businesses whose dispatchers coordinate changes through several separate tools. A **needs-based segment** groups customers around an improvement they need in their circumstances. The need might be resolving frequent conflicts while preserving control over assignments.

Behaviour and need are related but different. Two dispatchers may both edit appointments frequently. One is managing urgent repair work; the other is correcting inaccurate information entered elsewhere. A common action does not prove a common cause or make the same solution appropriate. Investigate what the action accomplishes and why it recurs.

Combine descriptors when they clarify the decision. A business with a dedicated dispatcher, frequent same-day changes and technicians working within a connected service area gives Cedar a more useful starting hypothesis than “small and medium-sized businesses”. The definition identifies a workflow and an operational difficulty, while leaving room to test whether headcount is a useful boundary.

Avoid turning observed behaviour into an identity. Someone who rarely uses an application may have infrequent need, lack permission, depend on another person's records or find the application unhelpful. “Low-engagement customer” describes a measure; it does not explain the person. The segment should help you investigate those alternatives rather than prematurely choose one.

The same reasoning applies to consumer products. A travel-planning tool could group people by age, but whether a trip includes several households making joint decisions may better explain a need for shared planning. Demographics can help describe a population; the decision determines whether they are the most useful basis for the product offer.

Choose variables the organisation can recognise with reasonable effort. “Businesses suffering coordination anxiety” may sound insightful but gives a researcher or salesperson little basis for identifying a suitable organisation. “A dispatcher must contact several people whenever a confirmed visit changes” describes an investigable condition. It still needs careful questioning: the contact may be quick and satisfactory. Recognisability helps recruit and compare cases; it does not prove that the observed arrangement is a problem.

## Turn a group into a target hypothesis

A **target market** is the group the organisation chooses to prioritise for an offer and the work needed to deliver it. It is a choice about attention and resources. It need not imply that nobody else can use the product, but it should make some design and commercial decisions easier.

For Cedar, a plausible candidate is service businesses where a dedicated dispatcher handles frequent appointment changes, technicians depend on centrally coordinated information, and the company wants clearer coordination while retaining human control. The existing alternative is a combination of schedules, messages and dispatcher knowledge. Roughly ten to fifty technicians could be an initial recruitment range, provided research checks the actual working conditions.

This is a segment hypothesis, not an established strategic decision. It contains several claims to investigate: that the problem is important, that the group can be reached, that buyers have reason and authority to pay, and that Cedar can serve them without taking on obligations it cannot sustain.

The hypothesis also identifies likely non-fit situations. A two-person contractor coordinating directly may have too little of the proposed problem. An enterprise operator requiring extensive client-specific controls may need an offer Cedar cannot presently support. Those are reasons to examine fit, not judgements about which customer is more valuable as a person or business.

Segment attractiveness requires evidence about several connected questions. Is the improvement important enough to prompt action? How does the buyer currently respond? What would adoption involve? Can Cedar reach and support enough suitable organisations on workable terms? A large number of companies in a database does not answer those questions.

Investigate contrasting cases. A company that fits the headcount range but rarely changes appointments can challenge the proposed size boundary. A smaller company with complex daily coordination can challenge the exclusion. A dispatcher who wants the improvement but cannot secure buying approval reveals a difference between user need and purchase conditions.

These comparisons help refine the segment without treating every exception as a reason to abandon grouping. A useful segment need not contain identical customers. It needs enough commonality to support coherent decisions, with important differences understood. If nearly every prospect requires a different core workflow, the proposed grouping may be too broad or the shared need too weak.

Choosing a target has consequences beyond marketing. Cedar would prioritise coordination and change handling, design support around the dispatcher relationship and assess pricing against the customer's working pattern. Sales would need to recognise when a prospect does not fit. A segment that changes only the wording of an advertisement has not yet guided the product.

Record what would change the choice. If suitable businesses generally solve changes adequately with existing tools, the proposed benefit may be weak. If the need is strong but each accounting connection requires substantial bespoke work, delivery economics may make the group difficult to serve. If another group shares the need and can adopt with less effort, it deserves comparison. These possibilities connect targeting with the economic questions already examined without pretending that a precise segment description settles them.

## Explain why this option fits

**Positioning** concerns how an offering is understood in relation to the intended customer's needs and alternatives.[^c15-n03] The organisation can choose the position it intends to communicate. It needs evidence to know whether customers actually understand or believe that position.

A **category** or **frame of reference** helps people recognise what kind of offering they are considering. Calling Cedar “a shared calendar” invites comparison with familiar calendar tools. Calling it “field-service coordination software” suggests responsibility for more of the working process. Either frame creates expectations about capability, price and effort. The wording should help the right customer understand the offer accurately.

A broader category is not automatically stronger. If Cedar presents itself as a complete enterprise operations system, a buyer may reasonably expect controls or implementation services that Cedar does not provide. If it presents itself only as a diary, a dispatcher may miss the reason to consider its job records and communication features. The frame should fit both the customer's problem and what Cedar can deliver.

**Differentiation** is a meaningful distinction from relevant alternatives. A capability is not useful differentiation merely because the product team built it differently. The customer must care about the difference, be able to recognise it and have a reason to believe it. The relevant alternative may be the present spreadsheet arrangement rather than another specialist vendor.

A **value proposition** states the benefit the offering proposes for a specified customer and gives a credible reason to consider it. For the candidate segment, an internal working proposition could be:

> Cedar brings schedules, job records and customer communication into one working arrangement for businesses whose dispatchers coordinate changing repair visits. The aim is to help staff follow the same information while the dispatcher retains responsibility for assignments.

The first sentence names baseline capabilities. The second describes an intended benefit that still needs investigation in the customer's environment. Before publishing stronger claims about dependable coordination, Cedar would need to examine how updates reach people, whether staff understand them and how the arrangement handles exceptions.

Contrast that wording with “the easiest way to eliminate scheduling problems”. Easiest compared with what, for which task and people? Eliminate which problems, under what conditions? A broad promise conceals evidence requirements and can attract customers whose expectations the product cannot meet.

Positioning should connect product, sales and support. If the sales message promises dispatcher control, the workflow should make that control meaningful and the support team should understand what has been promised. Repeatedly explaining away the headline during onboarding is a signal to examine the offer and message together.

Ask a prospective customer to explain the offer in their own words and describe when they would consider it. Their response can reveal an unexpected comparison or a missing reason to change. Agreement that a sentence “sounds good” provides less insight into whether the person recognises their situation and understands the proposed difference.

## Test the group and the promise

Different claims require different evidence. A demonstration can establish that a screen supports a particular action. Observation can reveal how a dispatcher incorporates that action into work. A comparison can investigate whether the action helps against the current alternative. A conversation about buying can reveal who must approve the purchase. None alone establishes every part of the value proposition.

Maintain a short claim-to-evidence map. For “shows appointment changes in one place”, inspect the actual capability and its limits. For “reduces coordination work”, examine the work before and after under a suitable evaluation design. For “preferred by dispatchers”, investigate actual preferences with a relevant sample and context. Do not let an accurate feature description quietly become proof of a broader outcome.

Involve a researcher when the targeting decision depends on whether a sample represents a wider population or whether a test distinguishes competing explanations. Bring the proposed segment, the decision and the evidence you already have. Work with commercial and finance colleagues on reachability and service economics. A useful segment description gives those specialists a clearer question; it does not establish the answers.

AI can generate candidate segments and alternative wording once the evidence and unknowns are supplied:

> Given these workflow notes, propose three ways to group customers that could change our product decisions. Separate observed differences from hypotheses. For each candidate, name the relevant alternative, a likely non-fit case and evidence that could disconfirm the group. Then draft plain positioning language using only supported capabilities. Do not invent market size, willingness to pay or customer reactions.

Check which source supports each proposed distinction. If the assistant labels a group “highly attractive”, ask what evidence establishes importance, reachability, buying conditions and delivery economics. If it predicts that customers will find a phrase reassuring, treat that as a wording hypothesis. Fluency can improve a draft without revealing what an audience actually perceives.

Take one broad customer label in your own organisation. Write two situations it conceals, including each person's task, current alternative and constraint. Propose a segment that would justify a different product decision. Identify one case that should not fit and one case that might challenge the boundary.

Then write a value proposition without a superlative or an unsupported number. Underline every claim requiring evidence and state how you would investigate it. The exercise succeeds when a colleague can identify whom the offer is for, why they might consider it and what remains uncertain. A polished slogan with no identifiable customer decision has not completed the work.

Cedar's target hypothesis can now guide investigation rather than stand in for it. Product judgement improves when “our customer” becomes a description of particular people, working arrangements and choices, and when the promise made to them stays connected to what the product can support.
