# Chapter 43: Building a Map of What You Don't Know

## Familiar words can conceal unfinished learning

Cedar's product manager can now follow a conversation about restoration claims, scopes of work and supporting records. That progress is useful. It does not yet answer whether Cedar should build a documentation capability for restoration contractors. Knowing the vocabulary, understanding one workflow and having enough evidence for an investment are different achievements.

The next question is not simply “What else should I read?” It is “What do we need to understand before this particular commitment, and what kind of understanding is missing?” An unresolved legal interpretation, an uncertain market estimate and an untested integration behave differently. They should not share one undifferentiated label of research needed.

**Metacognition** concerns monitoring and directing your own thinking and learning. Flavell's account distinguishes activities aimed at making cognitive progress from activities aimed at assessing that progress.[^c43-n01] In product work, the practical distinction is between reading another explanation and checking whether you can use the explanation to reason accurately about the decision.

Try explaining the proposed handoff without your notes. Identify who creates the record, who receives it, what decision it supports and what authority the recipient has. Where the explanation becomes vague, mark the gap. The exercise tests the usefulness of your understanding rather than the familiarity of the terms.

Also ask whether the team has evidence that you personally have not understood, or whether the evidence is missing altogether. The first problem may call for a specialist explanation. The second may require observation, investigation or a narrower commitment. Confusing them can lead to repeated research when a conversation would suffice, or confident action when the relevant fact has never been established.

A **knowledge map**, sometimes called an uncertainty map, records these distinctions around a decision. It can be a page, annotated diagram or set of linked notes. Its purpose is to make the state of understanding actionable. The format matters less than whether someone can identify the important question, the current basis for an answer and the next useful step.

Keep the map small enough to use. Include information that affects a choice, an important risk or another question's interpretation. A complete-looking document can obscure the decisive gap just as effectively as an empty one if every item receives equal attention.

## Give claims a status before relying on them

In a working map, “known facts” should identify factual claims supported well enough for the present purpose. Record each claim, its supporting evidence and its scope. “This contractor uses this record in this workflow” is narrower than “restoration contractors use this record”. Verification of the first does not establish the second.

An **assumed fact** is better described as an assumption being used as a factual premise. Cedar might assume that the same organisation receives every documentation package. Keep that premise visible until the evidence supports it. The label assumed does not mean the proposition is false; it means the present reasoning depends on something not adequately established.

A **model** connects selected features of reality so that the team can reason about them. Cedar's workflow diagram is a model. Even when its individual events are real, the sequence may omit exceptions or imply a dependency that has not been checked. Attach uncertainties to the relevant link rather than assigning one confidence label to the entire diagram.

An **estimate** approximates an unknown quantity. A market-size estimate can combine a count of businesses with assumptions about eligibility, need and willingness to adopt. Preserve those inputs and ranges. Repeating the final number in several presentations does not turn the estimate into a known market fact.

An **unresolved question** names what the team needs to determine. A **contested claim** has competing accounts or interpretations that matter. An **evidence gap** is missing support needed to assess a claim. These can overlap, but they lead to different actions: investigate a question, compare the bases of a disagreement or obtain the missing observation.

Some conclusions require **expert-dependent judgement**. A product manager can collect the relevant documents and understand the issue while still needing a qualified professional to interpret obligations or assess a restoration risk. Mark the decision and the required competence, not just the name of someone to ask casually.

For Cedar, a useful first map might look like this:

| Area | Current status | Next useful action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Terminology | Common meanings understood; local uses of approved remain ambiguous | Check the term against actual decisions and records |
| Regulatory requirements | Claims about applicability remain unverified | Identify jurisdiction/activity and obtain qualified interpretation of primary rules |
| Market size | Estimate depends on an uncertain eligible-customer definition | Check the population and assumptions before refining the total |
| Workflow | One documented sequence; wider applicability assumed | Observe a different relevant contractor and the receiving party |
| Technical feasibility | Required record access and permission behaviour unknown | Ask an engineer for a bounded investigation |
| Specialist assessment | Some proposed outputs may imply professional conclusions | Ask the relevant domain professional to assess scope and wording |

The table does not claim that these questions have been answered. It separates the state of knowledge from the work needed to improve it. Add the decision consequence and owner where doing so helps colleagues act.

Attach important entries to something another colleague can inspect: an approved research note, a source passage, a sample record or a specialist's stated assessment. Keep access appropriate to the material. A reference saying “discussed with operations” is difficult to challenge or reuse; a dated account of the specific workflow and its limits preserves more of the learning.

Separate disagreement about the world from disagreement about the product choice. Two colleagues can accept the same evidence and prefer different commitments because one gives more weight to operational risk. Marking their disagreement as an evidence gap would send the team searching for data that cannot settle the underlying preference. The next action is to make the trade-off and decision authority explicit.

Avoid treating confidence as a decorative colour. A confident participant can be wrong; a cautious specialist can have strong evidence. Record why the claim deserves reliance and what could undermine it. If a claim changes status, preserve the evidence or reasoning behind the change so that the map becomes a record of learning rather than a repeatedly overwritten summary.

## Break the decision into questions with dependencies

A **question tree** breaks a broad question into smaller questions whose answers help resolve it. Begin with the actual choice: should Cedar investigate, build or expand a particular documentation capability for a defined group of contractors? Those stages require different depth of evidence.

One branch asks whether the capability would address a consequential problem. That branch includes who performs the work, how often the difficulty occurs, what it costs and what alternatives already exist. Another asks whether Cedar can deliver the capability responsibly. It includes access to records, permissions, technical feasibility, professional boundaries and relevant obligations.

A third branch concerns whether the opportunity fits Cedar's direction and resources. This includes the reachable customer group, adoption effort, commercial arrangements and continuing support. The branches connect UX, software and economics without requiring a single person to become the specialist in each.

Keep the questions answerable. “Understand regulation” is too broad. “Which activities in the proposed workflow need qualified review before Cedar commits to them?” is a more useful starting question. A specialist can then help identify the relevant legal questions and evidence, rather than being asked to approve an undefined business idea.

Identify **prerequisite knowledge**: what must be understood before another answer can be interpreted. Cedar cannot assess whether an approved status may be shared before it knows what the status means, who asserted it and what sharing is intended to do. Likewise, a market estimate cannot be refined sensibly before defining which contractors and use cases are included.

Order the learning accordingly. Verify the workflow's meaning before estimating the engineering effort to reproduce it. Establish whether a proposed activity belongs within the product's intended role before designing its interface. These dependencies can prevent substantial work on a question that later proves irrelevant.

Distinguish a prerequisite from a preference for completeness. The team does not need to master every insurance product before observing a contractor's documentation process. It does need enough context to avoid misreading the records and enough safeguards to conduct the inquiry appropriately. Ask what minimum understanding makes the next investigation useful and responsible.

The tree can also reveal duplication. Three colleagues may separately ask whether contractors want automation when the unanswered issue is what information a receiving organisation accepts. Consolidate the repeated question and direct effort at the missing branch.

Do not assume the branches are independent. A change in the target contractor group can alter the workflow, market estimate and legal questions together. When an important premise changes, follow its connections through the map. Updating only the most visible number leaves the rest of the decision resting on an obsolete model.

## Look for questions the current map cannot see

**Unknown unknowns** are relevant matters the team has not yet recognised as unknown. You cannot make a complete inventory of them by definition. You can create conditions in which previously unrecognised questions are more likely to appear.

Look for anomalies: records that do not fit the diagram, people missing from the expected sequence, repeated exceptions and professionals using the same word differently. Cedar's map may contain only contractor and insurer roles, while a walkthrough reveals another party whose permission affects the handoff. The discovery changes what the team needs to investigate.

Seek perspectives beyond the people who designed the current process or want the proposed product. Someone handling disputed records may notice failure modes invisible in a successful demonstration. A contractor that declined the proposed workflow may reveal a condition absent from enthusiastic participants. Treat these perspectives as sources of questions and evidence, without assuming any one is representative of the whole field.

Use AI to ask repeatedly:

> What important question would someone competent in this field ask that we have not asked?

Supply the bounded decision and current map. Ask the assistant to explain why each suggested question matters, which existing assumption it challenges and what type of source or specialist could answer it. This makes a generated list easier to evaluate than a catalogue of everything that might conceivably go wrong.

Verify the suggestions. A model may introduce a rule from another jurisdiction, confuse a claims decision with a contractor's record or invent an institution. Check applicability and source existence before adding a suggestion as a real requirement. A plausible but unverified concern can remain a question; it should not silently become a constraint.

Invite a specialist to identify the map's most misleading omission. Show enough product context for the answer to be relevant. Ask whether a contested claim reflects different facts, different scopes or a professional interpretation. The map should retain the distinction rather than count expert opinions as votes.

Use each discovery to update dependencies. If Cedar learns that the intended recipient cannot access the proposed record under the current arrangement, interface polish is no longer the immediate issue. The next learning task concerns the arrangement, a different recipient or another product boundary. Detecting that change early is the practical value of monitoring what you do not know.

## Learn until the next decision is adequately supported

Research can have **diminishing returns**: additional effort produces progressively less useful information for the decision. Reading a fifth summary based on the same underlying source may add familiarity without reducing a consequential uncertainty. A short conversation about a missing handoff may add much more.

Review the learning queue using consequence, dependency and the likelihood that obtainable evidence can change an action. For Cedar, clarification of a proposed professional judgement may precede market sizing because it determines which capability is actually being considered. A technical investigation may proceed alongside workflow research if each can inform the next commitment without waiting for the other.

Periodically test whether the map describes your understanding accurately. Pick an important claim and explain what supports it, where it applies and what would make you revise it. Ask a colleague to challenge the explanation using an unfamiliar example. If you can repeat the definition but cannot recognise when it fails to apply, further learning should focus on examples and feedback rather than additional summaries.

Use that check to choose depth. A product manager who repeatedly interprets data-access decisions may need stronger foundations in permissions and information flows. A one-off question about a specialised restoration assessment may be better handled through a qualified professional. The map can reveal recurring skill needs without turning every expert-dependent item into an instruction to teach yourself the profession.

Set a **stop condition** for the current stage. Before approving further discovery, Cedar may need enough understanding to define a responsible research scope and reach relevant participants. Before building, it needs stronger evidence about the proposed workflow, feasible data use and necessary professional review. Before expansion, it needs evidence from actual use and operation. One stage's sufficiency does not establish the next stage's readiness.

A stop condition can state that the main alternatives are understood, critical assumptions have been checked to the required standard, remaining uncertainties are explicit and the next commitment is proportionate to them. It can also identify a deadline or resource limit. These limits do not waive an unresolved safety or legal requirement; they may mean narrowing or deferring the proposed action.

When evidence is unobtainable, record why and decide what follows. The product may need a smaller scope, a reversible test, a specialist assessment or no further investment. “More research” should not become an indefinite substitute for acknowledging that the current proposal cannot be supported.

For practice, take three statements beginning “We do not understand…”. Rewrite each as a specific uncertainty tied to a decision. Classify the current claim, identify prerequisite knowledge and choose an action suited to the gap. Make at least one action different from reading or asking AI.

Then write the possible finding that would change your next commitment and a condition for stopping the inquiry. Explain which uncertainty will remain after stopping and how the decision accommodates it. A useful learning agenda makes the next action clearer while preserving the limits of what that action can establish.

The map should evolve as the product manager learns. Its success is not measured by the number of entries marked complete. It is measured by whether the team notices consequential gaps, obtains the right help and makes commitments that its current understanding can support.
