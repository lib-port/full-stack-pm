# Chapter 45: The Product Manager as a Learning System

## Let the next question change your understanding

At the beginning of Cedar's story, the scheduling screen held the correct appointment time while the plumber followed an old printed schedule. Understanding the feature was insufficient to explain the failed visit. The product manager needed a wider account of communication, agreement and action, without inventing a technical cause that the evidence had not established.

The questions continued to change. An automatic reminder proposal required a comparison with the dispatcher's existing calls. Different service businesses revealed different coordination and buying needs. Operational failures showed why a responding server did not establish a dependable dispatch service. A proposed AI summary introduced another distinction: a fluent account of work could misstate what the technician had actually recorded.

Cedar's product choices developed with that understanding. Reminders moved from a proposal to a later launch whose apparent effect still needed causal investigation. Strategy concentrated attention on service businesses with roughly ten to fifty technicians. AI adoption required deciding which contribution to attempt, what evidence would support it and which actions should remain outside the system's authority. None of these choices removed the need to learn.

A product manager needs a practice that can repeat this adjustment. Curiosity starts the process, but useful curiosity is specific: what happened, whose situation matters, which explanation fits and what would change the next commitment? The purpose is to improve action through better understanding.

One recurring loop brings the disciplines together:

**Observe → frame → model → investigate → decide → act → measure → update.**

The arrows do not require a rigid sequence. A surprising observation can reopen the frame; a technical investigation can change the available actions. The loop is an account of responsibilities that must connect. Observation without a decision can become endless research. Action without measurement loses a chance to learn. Measurement without updating can become reporting that leaves the original beliefs untouched.

The product manager is part of this learning system. Their assumptions, methods and skills should change alongside the product. A useful model today may become incomplete when the customer group, operating conditions or product capability changes. Treat that possibility as a reason to remain correctable, not as a reason to avoid commitment.

## Connect observation to an investigable explanation

**Observe** the work and its consequences. UX methods help reveal what people do, how they understand the product and where the observed activity differs from the intended flow. Operational records help reveal failures that a demonstration hides. Commercial conversations can expose buying conditions that are absent from a usability session.

For Cedar, an owner requesting automation and a dispatcher protecting appointment promises offer different information. Keep the accounts linked to their roles and circumstances. Neither automatically represents every user or answers how the proposed system should behave.

**Frame** the problem around the improvement sought, the people affected, the current alternative and the constraints. This connects experience with economics and ethics. Reducing dispatcher work matters, but so do the homeowner's agreement, the technician's practical day and the costs borne by the service company and Cedar.

A frame should make alternatives possible. If the problem is unreliable coordination, an AI scheduler is one possible response, alongside clearer information, better change communication or a different division of responsibility. A frame that names only the preferred solution makes investigation less able to change the decision.

**Model** the relevant relationships. Human behaviour, information flow, software state, incentives and commercial arrangements all contribute. Use a representation detailed enough for the decision: perhaps the sequence from an appointment change to technician acknowledgement, or the assumptions connecting a new capability with adoption and retained revenue.

Mark the uncertain links. A customer saying a feature is attractive does not establish adoption; adoption does not establish continued value; continued use does not establish profitable delivery. Markets and business models help explain who can buy, what they compare and what Cedar must sustain. Engineering helps explain what the proposed behaviour depends on.

**Investigate** the uncertainty that most affects the choice. Observe a difficult workflow, inspect relevant records, test an interaction, run a bounded technical investigation or ask a qualified specialist. Decision science helps determine whether the information can change an action and whether obtaining it is worth the cost and delay.

AI can assist with explanations, alternative models and critiques of the investigation. Its contribution remains material to assess. Generated users do not supply observations, a generated causal explanation does not establish an effect and a plausible technical account does not replace the actual system's documentation or behaviour.

At this stage, progress means a better-supported choice or a clearer reason to narrow, defer or stop. The investigation can be successful even when it rules out the original proposal. What matters is that its answer reaches the decision before the organisation commits as though the uncertainty had already been resolved.

## Connect commitment to consequences and revision

**Decide** what to do using the available evidence, alternatives and constraints. Compare the best use forgone, the consequences of being wrong and the opportunity to reverse or stage the commitment. Strategy helps determine which customers and capabilities deserve sustained attention. Appropriate decision authority makes the commitment accountable.

For Cedar, concentrating on a particular service-business segment changes which requests deserve investigation. It does not prove that every feature for that segment is worthwhile. Likewise, a favourable expected value can support a trial without making every downside acceptable. Preserve the judgement behind the decision, including the uncertainties being accepted.

**Act** in a form proportionate to that understanding. A limited trial, a repair, a design change and a broad release are different commitments. Engineering, design, support and commercial work must agree on what is being introduced and how people will be helped when it fails.

The proposed job-history summary makes this concrete. A system that reads authorised notes and drafts a summary has a different responsibility from one that changes an appointment or tells a homeowner a repair is complete. Adopting AI should involve explicit choices about input, evaluation, human review and action authority, rather than a gradual expansion hidden inside a helpful interface.

**Measure** whether the intended change and important side effects occur. Use measures that represent the relevant outcomes, with operational definitions and checks on the data. Include guardrails where one group's improvement could create another group's burden. Qualitative investigation remains useful when the numbers do not explain the work.

Cedar's increasing scheduled-job count could conceal repeated bookings and cancellations. A decline in no-shows after reminders launched could reflect the feature, changing conditions or both. Measurement supplies observations; causal reasoning determines what those observations justify saying about the intervention.

**Update** the part of the model the evidence actually addresses. If a test reveals that a summary loses the difference between recommended and completed work, revise the intended use, evaluation or design. Do not conclude that every AI task is unsuitable. If a trial participant can use a feature, do not infer that the broader market will adopt it profitably.

Follow consequential updates through their dependencies. A changed customer segment can alter onboarding, permissions, pricing and support. A newly understood technical constraint can change both cost and user experience. Learning has not reached the product until the affected decisions and working arrangements change too.

Check whether the update reached everyone whose work depends on it. If a trial shows that a capability needs more setup support, the commercial promise, onboarding plan and cost estimate should reflect that finding. Updating only the research report leaves the product operating under the old assumption. The product manager can make the connection by identifying the affected decisions and asking their owners to confirm the revised basis.

Some evidence calls for another observation rather than an immediate design change. An isolated difficulty may reveal a serious failure mode, a local exception or a recording problem. Preserve its significance while investigating which explanation fits. Learning requires a response proportionate to the evidence, including restraint when the cause remains unclear.

The loop also operates during maintenance and retirement. Evidence can show that a capability costs more to sustain than its current benefit justifies. Responsible action may then mean reducing scope or supporting a transition. Continuous learning does not imply continuous feature expansion.

## Preserve enough of the decision to learn from it

A short **decision journal** records what you believed before the result was known. Include the question, alternatives, evidence, assumptions, expected consequences, important uncertainties and review point. For consequential forecasts, define the event and time horizon so that the prediction can later be resolved.

The record need not become another elaborate reporting system. Its value is that future reflection can distinguish what was known, what was assumed and what changed. Without that record, a convincing account of the outcome can replace the reasoning that actually produced the decision.

Use an **after-action review** to compare expectation with experience. Ask what was intended, what happened, why the difference matters and what should change next time. Include people who experienced the consequences, not only those who approved the work. An incident can reveal missing operational knowledge that never reached the original planning discussion.

Separate decision quality from outcome luck. A bounded experiment can sensibly produce an adverse result that the plan allowed for. A poorly supported commitment can succeed. Examine whether the process used available evidence well and handled uncertainty responsibly, alongside examining the outcome.

Review **calibration** across suitable, resolvable forecasts. Are events assigned high probabilities occurring at an appropriate frequency? Are ranges consistently too narrow? Small numbers of forecasts and shared causes limit what you can infer. The purpose is to improve future judgement, not to reward confidence or punish people for acknowledging uncertainty.

Build an accumulating evidence record. Retain source, context, method and important exceptions so that later colleagues can judge relevance. Several summaries of the same interview are one underlying observation. A new study of a different group may add information that repetition cannot. Keep contradicted claims visible with their correction rather than silently replacing them.

Reflection should produce a concrete change: a different recruitment approach, a clearer release condition, a revised forecast assumption or earlier specialist involvement. Name who will make the change and where its effect can be observed. Otherwise, the review may produce agreement without altering the next decision.

Revisit useful lessons when the context changes. A finding from a small trial is not permanently valid for a larger population. An operating rule that worked before a new integration may require revision afterwards. Evidence accumulates into better judgement when its conditions remain visible and its relevance is reconsidered.

## Maintain foundations and deepen where the work demands it

Broad foundations make unfamiliar problems recognisable. Keep enough UX knowledge to question a claim about users, enough economics to see a forgone alternative, enough software knowledge to trace information and failure, and enough market understanding to distinguish interest from a viable offer. Decision science connects these perspectives under uncertainty. AI can assist the investigation, while specialists supply depth and professional judgement where required.

Choose deeper learning where a topic is consequential, recurring and closely connected to your decisions. A product manager repeatedly assessing integrations benefits from stronger understanding of identity, permissions and data semantics. A rare specialised legal interpretation may call for qualified advice rather than a personal attempt to master the profession. Time and access to expertise belong in that choice.

Develop skills through use and feedback. Explain a concept without notes, apply it to an unfamiliar case and ask someone competent to challenge the reasoning. Return to the concept after time has passed. In Karpicke and Roediger's vocabulary-learning experiment, repeated retrieval improved delayed recall compared with dropping learned items from further testing.[^c45-n01] That result supports a bounded learning principle; recalling definitions alone does not establish product judgement.

Use AI as a practice partner. Supply a trusted explanation and ask for a new example, a boundary case and questions that reveal misunderstanding. Answer before reading the generated explanation. Check corrections against the source or a knowledgeable colleague, especially when the issue affects consequential decisions.

Keep the practice connected to real work. After learning a concept, identify a decision it changes or a question it helps you ask. If the only result is more terminology in your notes, investigate whether the learning has become usable. Protect sensitive information when using work examples, and use invented details where the purpose is practice rather than evidence.

Maintain relationships that make correction possible. A researcher who knows the product's context, an engineer willing to explain a failure and a domain professional who can challenge an interpretation are part of your capacity to learn. Bring them concrete questions, share what changed after their advice and preserve the boundaries of their contribution. Breadth becomes more useful when it connects you to depth you do not personally possess.

Make room for curiosity beyond the immediate task, while distinguishing exploration from decision evidence. Reading about another industry or examining an unfamiliar product can suggest an analogy. Before acting on it, identify which mechanisms transfer and which conditions differ. The useful result may be a new question rather than a ready-made answer. This keeps broad learning alive without turning every interesting idea into a requirement.

For a sustainable routine, choose one live decision and close a small learning cycle. Record an assumption, investigate it, make the next proportionate commitment and schedule a review. At that review, update both the product model and one aspect of your own practice. Keep the routine light enough to repeat during ordinary work.

The aim is not omniscience. It is the ability to recognise when your current understanding is insufficient, find the next useful question and obtain the evidence or expertise that question requires. The best product manager is not the person who always knows the answer. It is the person whose process reliably converts uncertainty into increasingly good judgement.
