# The AI-Augmented Product Owner

## Answers are easier to produce

AI assistants make specialized information and analysis easier to obtain through everyday language. They can explain unfamiliar ideas, summarize material, draft analyses, suggest alternatives, write code, and model a problem or its costs. You can begin by examining a proposed answer instead of creating every part of it yourself. For some tasks, this reduces the time needed to prepare material. Validating the proposal, implementing it, and operating the resulting service still require separate work.

A 2023 experiment with management consultants illustrates the distinction. Participants using the tested AI assistant worked faster on selected product-innovation tasks and produced work that received higher quality ratings. On a different business-analysis task, however, AI-assisted participants were less likely to reach the correct recommendation.[^1] These findings apply to the tasks and system studied. They do not establish the cost or quality of your next product decision.

Consider a fictional public library. Nadia, the manager responsible for its borrowing service, is considering lockers that would let readers collect reserved books after the building closes. Readers currently collect their books from a staffed desk during opening hours. Nadia is deciding whether to investigate the locker option further. She is not authorizing installation.

Nadia gives an external AI assistant the current collection procedure and explains that she wants to explore after-hours collection. She asks it to compare the options. In this illustrative example, the assistant recommends lockers and claims that existing staff can load them without increasing their workload. Nadia has supplied no measurements of staff capacity, reader data, costs, or technical documentation. The recommendation appears complete because the assistant has filled a gap in the available information with an unsupported claim.

Nadia treats the workload claim as an assumption: the recommendation relies on it, but there is no adequate support for it in the information provided. *Evidence* is information that supports or challenges a claim. She asks Luis, the branch manager responsible for daily operations, to trace how reserved books would be prepared, loaded, and retrieved. His answer is still needed. The draft has suggested an option, but it has established neither demand nor operating cost.

This book argues that judgment becomes relatively more important as information and *generated expertise* become more abundant. Here, generated expertise means AI-produced explanations and analyses that resemble specialist work; that resemblance does not establish professional competence. Each plausible answer gives you more to assess. Does it address the decision? What supports it? Whose work would it change? You do not need to examine every possible answer. You need to recognize the questions whose answers could change your choice.

Nadia's immediate need is to understand the operational work. Other aspects of the locker proposal require different knowledge. Sound judgment therefore involves both recognizing unsupported claims and knowing which disciplines can help you assess a proposal.

## No one can specialize in everything

This book uses *product owner* broadly to mean someone with substantial accountability for decisions about a product's direction and development. You might be a product manager, founder, service manager, or someone newly taking on product responsibility. The term does not require a formal role in an agile framework. Accountability also does not give you sole authority. Nadia can prepare a recommendation while other people authorize procurement, approve building work, or conduct professional assessments.

The library's proposal draws on several disciplines. Understanding who struggles to collect reserved books requires knowledge of people's behavior and experience. A locker may be available after closing yet still be difficult for some readers to use. Nadia needs evidence about readers' circumstances and an accessibility assessment. Imagining a grateful reader provides neither. Operations helps her examine how staff work would change. Engineering helps establish whether the lockers can work with the relevant physical and software systems.

Economics helps compare the resources each option requires. Lockers could shift tasks between staff, create new tasks, or replace work done at the desk. Nadia has not established their overall effect. Alternatives worth considering include retaining desk collection or changing opening hours. Adoption raises a further question: how would readers learn about the new arrangement and decide to use it? Announcing a service does not establish that people need it.

Other decisions may require knowledge of markets, statistics, law, security, or a particular industry. The relevant disciplines and the attention each deserves depend on the decision. Your job includes identifying uncertainties that could rule out a proposal and questions that can wait. You need enough understanding to connect advice across disciplines: a technically feasible option may still require more staff time than the organization can provide.

No one can develop deep professional expertise in every field that product work touches. Trying to do so would leave little time to make or revisit decisions. Yet delegating every unfamiliar question can leave you unable to explain how the answers fit together. Broad foundations help you make those connections. Specialists provide the depth that particular assessments require.

Collaboration can begin while you are defining the problem. Luis can help Nadia identify operational questions before she prepares a detailed proposal. She can ask a specialist to explain a constraint and explore alternatives while respecting that specialist's authority to assess the issue. *Product judgment* means using context, evidence, relevant knowledge, and an understanding of your limits to decide what to investigate, what change to pursue, and when to involve others.

## A broad foundation, with depth when needed

The book's learning model combines three elements: **broad foundations + AI-assisted situational specialization + expert collaboration**.

- **Broad foundations** help you recognize the kind of problem you face and understand how an answer in one discipline affects a question in another.
- **AI-assisted situational specialization** means developing enough depth for a particular decision, using AI to support selected learning tasks.
- **Expert collaboration** brings in the deeper experience and judgment that specialists can provide.

These elements can work together from the outset. You can involve a specialist while you are learning the basics.

The aim is to become an *accelerated generalist*: someone who can develop understanding within a defined scope as a situation demands. AI may help you prepare and refine learning material more quickly. It does not promise mastery in an afternoon or an expert answer on demand. The practical goal is to contribute more usefully: identify a missing premise, explain the question, and understand how a specialist's answer affects the available options.

Foundations give new information something to connect to. The National Academies' synthesis of learning research describes how prior knowledge can support further learning but can also bias attention toward familiar interpretations.[^2] Knowledge of operations may help Nadia notice the workload assumption. She must still examine how the library actually works. Familiarity with an explanation does not establish that it fits this situation.

The book calls the understanding needed to participate usefully *minimum viable expertise*. At this level, you understand essential terms, recognize relevant issues, ask meaningful questions, notice plainly unsupported reasoning, and know where to investigate further. You can explain your uncertainty to a specialist and recognize when a decision needs their involvement. This is a working level of understanding. Reading an answer does not establish that you have reached it, and reaching it does not confer a professional qualification. The depth you need depends on the task and its consequences. Knowing what an accessibility assessment involves, for example, does not mean you can perform one competently.

Return to Nadia's unanswered question about staff workload. In a second request to the AI assistant, she describes the current desk procedure, the proposed lockers, the decision she faces, and the roles of Luis and the relevant specialists. She identifies the missing information about demand, capacity, cost, accessibility, security, and technical compatibility. She asks for no more than three questions, in priority order, each with a reason, an appropriate source to consult, and any assumption that remains untested. She also asks the assistant to explain unfamiliar terms and avoid inventing findings.

An illustrative response might propose these questions:

1. **How would preparing and loading lockers change staff workload?** The original recommendation depends on a claim about that workload. Ask Luis to trace the proposed steps and compare them with the current desk procedure. The assumption that lockers would add no work remains untested.
2. **Which readers need after-hours collection, and could they use the proposed lockers?** Demand and accessibility affect whether the service would be useful. Seek evidence from readers and an accessibility assessment. The assumption that lockers would meet readers' needs remains untested.
3. **What connections, if any, would the lockers need to the lending system?** Technical requirements affect whether the proposal is feasible. Review the system's documentation with a relevant engineer. Compatibility with the lending system remains an untested assumption.

These questions give Nadia a clearer agenda for deciding whether lockers deserve further investigation. They identify work to examine, needs to establish, and technical requirements to assess. They do not establish demand, staff capacity, accessible use, or compatibility. Nor do they support the original claim that lockers would add no work. Additional work and a need to hire are separate questions: Nadia would need to establish both the workload and the staff's capacity to absorb it. Luis's review of the work is the next relevant check.

A better request has clarified the investigation. It has not produced observations of readers or staff, inspected the lending system, or completed an assessment. Agreement from another AI response would not provide independent evidence for the workload claim. In a real investigation, use a tool your organization has approved and follow its requirements for sharing information. This example requires neither patron records nor access to live systems.

AI can amplify useful work and unsupported assumptions. A workload assumption copied into a proposal, budget, and communication plan may become more prominent without becoming better supported. AI can also make explanations and investigation questions easier to prepare and refine. Here, the useful result is a clearer investigation agenda. The locker decision remains open.

Accountability means explaining and reviewing your reasoning, respecting who has authority to decide, arranging appropriate checks, and responding to the consequences of a decision. Nadia remains responsible for bringing the findings together in her recommendation. She does not need to verify every engineering detail herself. The *specialist boundary* marks the point at which a task requires deeper experience, a professional qualification, authorized review, or specialist judgment. Recognizing that boundary helps you contribute while ensuring that people with the relevant expertise and authority make the assessments your recommendation depends on.

## What this book promises

*Product Judgment* provides foundations for recognizing problems, understanding explanations, applying ideas, investigating uncertainty, making tradeoffs, and collaborating. It draws on several disciplines. User experience, or UX, studies and shapes people's experience of a product: can they accomplish what matters to them? Economics examines how benefits, costs, and incentives affect a choice. Marketing examines whom a product serves, what alternatives people have, and how they discover and adopt it. Software engineering helps you understand what building, connecting, maintaining, and operating a system requires. Decision science helps you reason about evidence, uncertainty, and competing choices. AI is both a subject of study and a possible aid to investigation.

These questions arise throughout a product's life. Discovery and strategy help determine which problems and opportunities deserve attention. Design and delivery turn an intended change into something people can use. Launch introduces it; adoption makes it part of people's activities. Operation and maintenance involve the continuing work of keeping it useful. Products also evolve and are eventually retired. Each stage requires judgment, and changing circumstances may require you to revisit earlier decisions. Shipping a feature resolves only some of the questions.

The book emphasizes *durable knowledge*: principles, distinctions, and habits of reasoning that remain useful as tools and working arrangements change. Asking what work must change and who bears its cost is one such habit. The question matters whether readers collect reserved books from a desk or a locker, and regardless of which system records the loan. Distinguishing assumptions from evidence remains useful when the tool used to write the proposal changes.

*Transient knowledge* concerns a particular tool, version, vendor, policy, interface, or period. The location of an assistant's settings may be useful today and different tomorrow. An integration's documented behavior may determine whether a proposed connection is feasible. Check these details when your decision depends on them. The distinction helps you decide what to retain as a reasoning habit and what to look up again. Transient details can be essential; durable principles can still need correction.

The book aims to help you participate effectively while understanding the limits of your knowledge. It will not qualify you as an engineer, designer, economist, statistician, lawyer, security professional, or other specialist. Detailed coding, advanced statistics, deep machine-learning mathematics, and vendor-specific operations fall outside its scope. Nor can a book guarantee a sound decision or remove uncertainty. It can help you ask a more useful question, understand an explanation, and identify a justified next step, even when someone else must conduct the assessment or authorize the work.

You do not need to use a particular delivery framework, technology stack, or AI provider. AI is optional. Reading a source, observing work, calculating directly, or speaking with a specialist may be the better next step. Choose assistance to suit the question and the available information, then assess its contribution.

The approach applies to public services, internal tools, marketplaces, consumer products, and combinations of physical products and services. Many examples involve software because product decisions often involve it. Product responsibility also extends to the work and experiences around that software. A recurring case will connect the disciplines and help you relate the questions to your own setting.

## Meet Cedar

Cedar is a fictional operations platform for small and medium-sized field-service businesses, including maintenance companies, electricians, and facilities contractors. A service business can use it to schedule visits, keep job histories, and invoice for work. Several groups have a stake in how it works.

The owner or manager of a service business buys access to Cedar or manages its use, taking account of staffing, customers, profitability, and cash flow. A dispatcher or coordinator employed by the service business organizes visits and adjusts schedules. A technician performs the repair or maintenance work and may use Cedar on a mobile device. The service customer receives that work and needs clear communication and straightforward payment. The customer does not necessarily buy access to Cedar or log into it.

Cedar's product and business team makes decisions about the platform. Its members are distinct from the staff of the service businesses that use Cedar. A platform decision can affect each group differently: the buyer, the people organizing and performing the work, and the customer receiving the service.

Later chapters revisit Cedar to examine decisions from different disciplinary perspectives. The case is a recurring illustration. It makes the relationships easier to examine as your understanding develops; it does not describe an actual company's history or prove that a proposed change will succeed.

## How to use this book

The book moves from foundations to the disciplines that inform product decisions, then shows how to apply those disciplines together. You do not need specialist training to begin. Its eight parts offer a sequence for learning and a reference for questions that arise in your work.

| Part | Chapters | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I — Foundations of Product Judgment | 1–5 | Establish what a product includes, how to think about value, and how evidence, assumptions, and systems shape decisions. Introduce the generalist's approach to learning. |
| II — Understanding People and Experience | 6–10 | Examine behavior, evidence about users, problems, and interaction. Explore how accessibility, trust, and ethics affect people's experience. |
| III — Understanding Value, Markets, and Growth | 11–16 | Connect economics and incentives to alternatives, business models, positioning, and adoption. Examine who benefits and what makes continued use worthwhile. |
| IV — Understanding Software and Engineered Systems | 17–23 | Build an understanding of software, data, integration, architecture, reliability, security, and maintainability so you can participate in discussions about what a system requires. |
| V — Evidence, Uncertainty, and Decisions | 24–28 | Reason about uncertainty, measurement, causal evidence, bias, and competing choices. Assess how well an answer supports the decision you face. |
| VI — Working Effectively With AI | 29–34 | Examine capabilities, context, ways of working, verification, tool use, and AI product design. Distinguish working with an assistant from adding AI capabilities to a product. |
| VII — Integrated Product Practice | 35–41 | Combine the disciplines across discovery, strategy, solutions, delivery, launch, operation, and retirement. Trace how one product choice raises questions in other areas of work. |
| VIII — Becoming a Rapid Specialist | 42–45 | Enter unfamiliar domains, identify knowledge gaps, collaborate with specialists, and keep learning. Develop the depth your decision and responsibilities require. |

On a first reading, follow the chapters in order so that the foundations support the later discussions. AI appears throughout the book as well as in Part VI. That part examines AI itself; the other parts provide the knowledge needed to use and question its outputs. The chapters on integrated practice then bring the disciplines together around product decisions.

Later, use the book as a reference. Start with a decision or a gap in your understanding, find the relevant chapter, and revisit its foundations as needed. If you lack evidence about users, return to Chapters 7–8. To prepare for a technical discussion, consult the relevant chapter in Part IV and revisit Chapter 44 on collaboration. You can take a useful next step without rereading the whole book.

In either mode, try keeping one real decision in mind. Identify an unresolved question and apply the chapter's ideas to it. Would the answer change your proposed action? What information would help you assess that answer? You may find that a conversation or a source would be more useful than further analysis. This optional habit connects the reading to your work; it requires no journal or template.

Before continuing, set this Introduction aside and explain its argument in two or three sentences. Include broad foundations, AI-assisted learning for a particular situation, specialist collaboration, and the continuing need for evidence and accountability. Then choose a product decision. Identify an uncertainty, a task with a clear scope that AI could help with, a way to check its output, and a person or source you need to consult before acting. If your explanation treats an AI-generated answer as authorization to proceed, revisit Nadia's unresolved workload claim.

Chapter 1 begins with a question that underlies all these decisions: what does the product include? Before deciding how it should change, establish whose work and experience you intend to affect and what falls within the scope of your decision.
