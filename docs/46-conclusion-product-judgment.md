# Conclusion: Product Judgment in an Age of Abundant Intelligence

## What remains when answers multiply

An explanation can arrive before you have properly understood the question. A proposal can contain alternatives, calculations and confident recommendations before anyone has examined the work it would change. AI makes these forms of intellectual production easier to obtain: explanations, options, drafts, analyses, code, models, hypotheses and arguments. That can be useful. It also makes the decision about what to rely on part of the work from the beginning.

Return to Amira's proposal for automatic offers of cancelled swimming-lesson places. A persuasive account of fewer telephone calls would not tell her whether families could respond in time, whether reception would handle fewer exceptions or whether the booking system could keep an offered place available. Those questions concern an actual service and the people using it. Producing several versions of the proposal would not, by itself, answer them.

Amira can nevertheless make progress. She can ask Joel to trace the present reception work, arrange for an engineer to explain reservation behaviour and investigate families' circumstances. She can use an assistant to prepare questions, compare explanations and identify an assumption she overlooked. Each contribution has a purpose and a limit. The decision becomes better grounded as information about the service replaces convenient guesses.

The important change is in what happens between receiving an answer and acting on it. You can inspect its premises, identify the people whose experience is missing and choose a check that could alter the recommendation. You can also reject useful assistance by demanding a guarantee it cannot provide. Product judgement involves deciding how much confidence a particular action requires and what would justify that confidence.

An abundance of answers does not remove the need for this work. It gives you more material on which to practise it. A generated explanation may help you recognise a concept, a draft may make disagreement easier to see, and a model may expose a quantity that needs measurement. Their contribution depends on their connection to the decision. The cost of producing an answer and the value of relying on it are different questions.

This is a reason to take the opportunity seriously without handing it more authority than the evidence permits. Faster preparation can create room for better investigation. It can also move an unsupported assumption through several documents before anyone examines it. You decide which of those possibilities your working arrangements encourage, and you can change those arrangements when their results reveal a weakness.

## The complements an answer needs

Several things make an answer useful that an answer alone cannot supply. **Context** explains the situation: who is trying to do what, with which information and constraints. **Evidence** gives a claim support appropriate to its use. **Judgement** connects that support to alternatives, consequences and the decision at hand. A recommendation stripped of these relationships may sound sensible while addressing a different problem.

For Amira, context includes the reception process, the waiting list and the meaning of an offered place. Evidence could include observed work and records whose limitations she understands. Judgement is needed to decide whether a promising change warrants further investigation, a limited trial or a larger commitment. None of those steps follows automatically from finding an attractive idea.

**Priorities** determine which improvements deserve scarce attention. **Taste**, in product work, is a developed ability to recognise qualities that serve the intended purpose: clarity, coherence, appropriate restraint and care in the details people encounter. Taste needs correction through experience and evidence. An elegant interface can still conceal an allocation rule that people find confusing or unfair.

**Trust** concerns whether people have good reason to rely on what the product and its provider promise. A clear explanation of what has happened, a workable way to recover and an honest account of uncertainty can support that reliance. A polished statement that hides an important limitation can damage it. Trust should follow dependable conduct; it should not be treated merely as a feeling to produce through persuasive wording.

**Responsibility** includes recognising who bears the consequences of a proposed change. **Accountability** includes explaining the reasoning, respecting decision rights, arranging appropriate checks and responding when the result differs from the intention. You cannot discharge either obligation by pointing to a recommendation the software produced. Nor does accountability require you to perform every specialist assessment yourself.

**Real-world experience** reveals details that a simplified model may leave out. A dispatcher knows which apparently available technician is collecting a part. A receptionist knows what a family usually means by asking to keep a place. **Expert practice** adds disciplined methods, accumulated judgement and the ability to recognise limits within a field. These contributions can challenge a proposal at the point where a fluent explanation is least likely to expose its own missing premise.

The book's argument is that such complements become more important as intellectual output becomes easier to generate. They give abundant material direction and consequence. Investing in them means improving how people observe, discuss, verify, decide and learn together, as well as choosing which tools help with particular tasks.

## Broad knowledge gives depth a purpose

Broad foundations help you notice which kind of question needs answering. They make it possible to recognise that an apparent interface problem concerns incomplete information, that an adoption problem concerns switching work or that a pricing proposal changes incentives. Without that orientation, you may investigate the most visible part of the problem while leaving its decisive relationship untouched.

Depth then has a purpose. You learn more about a data model because two records cannot safely be treated as the same customer. You learn more about uncertainty because a decision depends on a forecast whose precision is misleading. You study a workflow because the proposed automation changes who must do the exception handling. The learning effort follows the question that could change the commitment.

AI assistance can support that effort through explanation, comparison, practice and criticism. Its usefulness depends on the material provided and the checks that follow. Asking for an accessible account can help you enter an unfamiliar subject. Explaining the idea yourself, applying it to a contrasting case and discussing the result with someone competent can reveal whether the apparent understanding survives use.

This is why broad knowledge matters in a world of sophisticated generated answers. You need enough understanding to recognise relevant omissions, connect advice across domains and choose where deeper investigation is worthwhile. You also need to notice when the decision requires somebody else's expertise or authority. Breadth makes those choices possible; it does not confer every qualification the work may require.

Specialists can participate from the beginning. A short conversation may correct the question before you spend hours investigating the wrong issue. Later, better preparation can help you explain the context and ask a precise follow-up. Strong collaboration combines active understanding with respect for the specialist's methods and limits. It avoids both passive acceptance of every recommendation and attempts to defeat inconvenient advice with generated counterarguments.

The aim is a repeatable ability to acquire the depth the next situation needs. Sometimes that means learning enough to recommend a change. Sometimes it means learning enough to recognise that the change needs a professional assessment. Sometimes it means finding that a familiar solution does not fit the actual problem. Each result can be useful if it leads to a more defensible next action.

You will continue to encounter unfamiliar domains. A foundation worth maintaining helps you approach them with questions you can refine, rather than confidence you must defend. Your understanding can grow through the work without requiring you to pretend that every uncertainty has disappeared.

## Turn understanding into a responsible commitment

Product responsibility concerns intentional change in systems that already contain people, habits, information and constraints. A feature enters that arrangement. Its consequences depend on what people do with it and on the other activities that support or frustrate their actions. Even a narrow software change can therefore require a broader investigation.

Cedar's saved appointment illustrates the point. The dispatcher records the homeowner's agreed time, but the plumber acts on an older printed schedule. The record is necessary information in one place. Coordinating the visit also requires that the right information reach the person who must act. Choosing a product boundary that excludes that transfer would hide the question the team needs to investigate.

A responsible commitment names what is expected to change and what remains uncertain. It makes the alternatives visible, explains the evidence and identifies the people who can authorise and assess the work. It also establishes how the organisation will notice an unwelcome consequence. These elements make a decision inspectable; they do not guarantee its outcome.

Acting under uncertainty requires proportion. A small, reversible trial may be justified with less evidence than an irreversible change affecting many customers. A proposal with a serious downside may require specialist review before even a limited trial. The relevant question is whether the information and controls are adequate for the commitment, given the consequences of being wrong.

Learning continues after action. A technically successful release may reveal unanticipated support work. A feature that helped one group may fit another group's circumstances poorly. A familiar measure may stop representing what matters after people adapt to it. Observe those changes and revisit the explanation, rather than treating the original plan as proof that the product is working as intended.

Continuing a product is also a commitment. It consumes attention, creates dependencies and promises some level of future care. Maintaining, narrowing, replacing and retiring an offering all deserve judgement. The responsibility extends to helping affected people understand a transition and addressing the records and work that remain dependent on it.

The purpose of disciplined learning is therefore action that can be explained and revised. Endless investigation can delay a useful change, just as premature confidence can create avoidable harm. A clear decision, an explicit stopping condition and a way to learn from the result help you navigate between those risks. They allow uncertainty to remain visible while useful work proceeds.

## Begin with the next consequential question

Choose a decision already present in your work. Describe it without beginning with a feature name or a preferred technology. State whose situation you want to improve, what those people do today and what would otherwise happen. Identify one assumption carrying more weight than its evidence currently supports.

Then ask what would change your next action. The answer may require a conversation, a source inspection, a calculation, observation of a task or a specialist assessment. Choose the check for its ability to inform the decision. A large analysis is not automatically more useful than a short investigation directed at the right uncertainty.

Use an AI assistant where its contribution is specific. It might help you formulate a question, explain an unfamiliar concept, identify a missing actor or challenge your interpretation. Give it the necessary context, preserve the source of consequential claims and examine the result. Keep the decision and its evidence visible while the tool performs its assigned part.

Record what you decided and why, including what you expected to happen and what would prompt reconsideration. Return to that account after the consequences become observable. Ask which part of your understanding improved, which assumption failed and which knowledge you need to develop next. The value of reflection lies in changing a future judgement, rather than producing a polished story about the past.

You do not need to know everything before taking responsibility. You need to recognise relevant limits, learn with a purpose and involve the people whose knowledge the decision requires. You need enough courage to act when the case warrants action and enough honesty to revise an explanation when the evidence changes.

The next useful question may be modest. Who receives the changed appointment? Which work does this cost estimate omit? What would a dissenting customer say? Which source supports the claimed obligation? What happens when the proposed action fails? Answering one such question carefully can change the product more usefully than generating another collection of attractive features.

Product judgement develops through these repeated acts of attention. Broad understanding helps you recognise the issue. Focused learning and expert collaboration help you investigate it. Evidence and context help you decide what to do. Responsibility carries the decision into the lives and work it affects, and brings what happens there back into your understanding.

That is the continuing task: to make intentional changes in complex systems, learn from their consequences and take care with what other people will come to depend on.
