# Chapter 36: Framing Products and Strategy

## Choose what the organisation will concentrate on

A solo contractor wants to start using Cedar without configuring staff roles. A growing service business wants its dispatchers to coordinate several technicians reliably. An enterprise facilities operator wants extensive integration and controls across many organisational units. Each request can make sense. Combining all three into a feature plan does not establish that Cedar can serve all three customers well with the resources it has.

Cedar chooses to concentrate on service businesses with roughly 10–50 technicians, rather than simultaneously pursuing solo contractors and enterprise facilities operators. This is a strategic choice to examine, not evidence that this segment guarantees success. The choice becomes meaningful through the product, commercial and operating commitments it changes.

**Strategy** is a coherent set of choices for addressing a consequential challenge under constraints. It explains whose situation the organisation intends to improve, why its approach could work, and how its actions and resources support that approach. The choices must be specific enough to guide decisions when several worthwhile requests compete.

An aspiration such as “be the most trusted service platform” expresses an ambition. A goal such as increasing retained revenue states a desired result. A roadmap sequences work, such as a permissions improvement followed by an accounting connection. Each can contribute to management, but none alone explains why these customers, this problem and this approach deserve the organisation's concentrated effort.

Richard Rumelt distinguishes a diagnosis of the challenge, an overall approach to addressing it, and actions that support one another. He also warns against treating goals or a long task list as a strategy.[^c36-n01] The value of this distinction is practical: a person reading Cedar's strategy should understand how to assess the next request, not merely know that leadership wants growth.

Suppose an enterprise prospect offers an attractive contract if Cedar builds a special approval hierarchy. The roadmap may have room to move dates. The strategic question comes first: does the request strengthen Cedar's chosen approach, or require a different product and service organisation? A sufficiently valuable opportunity could justify changing direction, but that would be a new choice to explain, not an innocent extra feature.

A roadmap can then express a strategic sequence. If dependable setup is the main obstacle, improving import and initial configuration may precede another scheduling capability. If the obstacle is unreliable information during the working day, a different sequence follows. Dates and feature names communicate the work after this reasoning; they cannot supply the missing explanation. When new evidence changes the sequence, colleagues should be able to see whether the underlying strategy remains intact or whether Cedar has changed the problem it intends to solve.

Product manager Maya should make this reasoning visible with leadership, engineering, sales and support. She cannot declare coherence on their behalf if their actual commitments differ. A written strategy matters when it helps those people make compatible decisions and identify where an exception requires reconsideration.

## Explain the diagnosis and intended advantage

A **diagnosis** is an explanation of the important conditions and obstacles. It selects what matters from a much larger picture. Cedar's working diagnosis is that some growing service businesses have coordination demands beyond informal arrangements, yet cannot sustain the implementation work and operating complexity of a much more extensive system. That diagnosis is a claim requiring evidence, not a truth supplied by the technician count.

Specify the customer situation. Several technicians work away from the office. Someone coordinates appointments and changes. The business needs reliable information across those roles, but may have limited capacity to administer elaborate software. Investigate where this description fits and where it fails. A ten-technician company with unusual contractual requirements may differ more from the target than a fifty-five-technician company with a familiar operating model.

The approximate 10–50 range helps focus recruitment, sales qualification and product decisions. It should not replace understanding the workflow. The target **problem** is dependable coordination of changing work, including the commitments people must preserve. Size helps locate possible customers; the problem explains why Cedar could matter to them.

Define the desired **outcomes** for those people. Dispatchers should spend less effort reconciling conflicting plans. Technicians should have usable information about the work they are expected to do. Service customers should experience more dependable appointments. For Cedar, the approach should support sustainable customer relationships and a service it can maintain. These outcomes require measurement and interpretation; announcing them does not establish that a chosen feature will cause them.

The **differentiated approach** might be reliable coordination that customers can adopt without a large implementation project. That is more specific than claiming Cedar will be easier, cheaper and more powerful than every alternative. It focuses attention on the whole transition and daily workflow: suitable defaults, clear changes, manageable configuration and support that understands dispatch work.

The relevant comparison includes existing spreadsheets, calls and memory, as well as other products. Cedar must preserve what those arrangements do well and improve something customers consider important enough to change for. A technically novel scheduler may offer little advantage if setup is difficult and ordinary exceptions remain confusing.

Harvard Business School's Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness presents distinctive customer value, choices about what to exclude, and compatibility among activities as central strategic ideas.[^c36-n02] In Cedar's case, the intended advantage must emerge from connected choices. No single adjective on the website can establish it.

Ask what would make a customer prefer this approach despite a rival having more features. Then ask what Cedar would have to do consistently to make that reason credible. The answers connect positioning to work the organisation must actually perform.

## Make the choices agree across the business

A focused product with an unfocused commercial model can still be incoherent. If sales promises extensive customisation to every prospect, engineering inherits obligations inconsistent with a manageable product. If pricing makes essential technician access difficult to justify, customers may undermine the shared information the strategy depends on.

The consequences of Cedar's focus span six connected areas:

| Area | Consequence of the chosen focus | Tension to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product | Support dispatchers and technicians through a complete coordination task | Extra options can obscure ordinary work |
| Pricing | Offer understandable terms for a multi-person service business | Charges must support access and cover relevant costs |
| Sales | Qualify for workflow fit and explain the transition | A large unsuitable deal can distort commitments |
| Engineering | Maintain dependable shared state and bounded configuration | Customer-specific exceptions can multiply change costs |
| Integrations | Support the connections central to the target workflow | Every additional connection creates continuing obligations |
| Support | Help customers establish and sustain workable dispatch practices | High-touch help must fit the economics of the offer |

The table is a set of implications to test, not a completed operating plan. In pricing, Cedar needs a charge customers can understand as staffing changes and one that supports the cost of serving them. A subscription with relevant usage charges remains possible, but the exact unit and terms require evidence. “Target medium-sized firms” does not determine a price.

Sales needs qualification questions that reveal fit. How are changes communicated today? Who administers the system? Which accounting connection is essential? What prevents adoption? Qualification should help Cedar decline or redirect a poor fit before a contract creates expectations. It also produces evidence about whether the chosen segment is commercially reachable.

Engineering needs to understand which variation is part of the intended offer. Supporting a clear set of booking rules may fit the strategy; accumulating a separate rule for every customer may undermine it. Reliability, permissions and maintainability become capabilities that support the commercial promise. Their work belongs in the investment plan even when it is less visible than a new screen.

Integration choices require particular discipline. A connection shared by many suitable customers can reduce repeated work and switching effort. A bespoke enterprise connection can consume the same people while serving a different operating model. Assess the workflow and long-term responsibility, not just the connector's initial implementation estimate.

Support closes the loop. If adoption requires substantial help, Cedar must include that work in its offer and cost model. Clear setup guidance, staff training and repeatable problem resolution could make that help more effective. Promising effortless onboarding while relying on unpriced individual rescue makes the apparent product advantage depend on hidden labour.

The **business logic** connects these choices: customers gain enough benefit to adopt and remain; Cedar can reach and support them at sustainable cost; repeatable capabilities serve enough suitable relationships to justify continued investment. Each link needs evidence. A rising customer count can coexist with poor economics if every account requires a new exception or disproportionate support.

## Treat capabilities and exclusions as commitments

A **required capability** is something the organisation must be able to do for its strategy to work. Cedar needs more than a list of functions. It may need dependable data import, useful access controls, expertise in dispatch operations, appropriate customer education and a way to maintain selected integrations. Some capabilities sit in software; others sit in people and operating routines.

Separate what exists from what must be acquired. If the approach depends on easy migration but Cedar cannot reconcile imported appointment records, migration is a strategic gap. Calling it an onboarding detail would hide an obstacle to the intended advantage. Leadership must fund the work, obtain help, narrow the promise or reconsider the approach.

Capabilities also take time to develop. Hiring a support specialist does not instantly create shared operating knowledge. Building one integration does not establish an ability to maintain several reliably. The strategy should describe a feasible path to the necessary capabilities and recognise what the organisation must limit while that path is incomplete.

Now state **choices not pursued**. Cedar will not optimise its near-term offer for a solo contractor who needs almost no staff coordination. It will not promise an enterprise facilities operator every bespoke approval structure or organisation-specific connection. Those customers may still find parts of Cedar useful, but their distinctive requirements will not automatically determine the product's direction.

This does not authorise neglect of existing customers. Commitments already made still need handling. A new focus may require a supported transition, a clearly bounded existing service or a separate decision about an obligation. Strategy concerns future choices in the context of real responsibilities, rather than erasing inconvenient relationships from a presentation.

Make exclusions concrete enough to resist attractive exceptions. “No enterprise complexity” is vague. “We will not undertake a customer-specific integration unless it supports an identified, repeatable target workflow and has a funded maintenance owner” supplies a decision rule. That rule may still permit an exception, but the exception has to explain its fit and cost.

The same discipline can reveal an overly narrow strategy. If several suitable customers need a capability Cedar excludes, investigate whether the exclusion rests on a mistaken diagnosis. A strategic boundary is a reasoned choice, not a device for dismissing contrary evidence. Keep the difference between changing an implementation and revising the intended customer relationship explicit.

A useful test is to ask what the organisation will stop doing so that the strategy receives resources. If every existing programme continues unchanged and every request remains eligible, the new focus may exist only in language. Opportunity cost becomes visible when people can identify which work will wait, which commitments will end and what the released capacity makes possible.

## Keep the strategy answerable to evidence

A strategy can be coherent and still be wrong. Cedar's plan rests on **assumptions**: enough businesses have the coordination problem; they can be reached; the proposed approach matters relative to alternatives; implementation and support costs are manageable; and the organisation can build the required capabilities. Coherence shows that the argument fits together. Evidence assesses whether its premises hold.

Record the assumptions that could materially change the choice. For each, identify the present support, the remaining uncertainty and a practical way to learn more. Evidence about buyer urgency differs from evidence about technician usability. A successful demonstration does not establish sustainable support cost. Avoid using one favourable signal to stand in for the whole argument.

**Risks** include more than a competitor releasing a feature. Cedar might attract businesses whose requirements systematically exceed the intended offer. Sales incentives might reward contracts that produce expensive custom work. Reliability problems might destroy the benefit of otherwise clear coordination. A low advertised price might recruit customers whose service needs the price cannot fund. Each risk names a mechanism that colleagues can investigate and manage.

Choose **measures** that test the strategic argument. Track whether suitable prospects reach a working setup, whether dispatchers and technicians use the relevant coordination flow, and whether the resulting work improves. Examine support demand and customer economics alongside adoption. Define populations, periods and what counts as success before treating a dashboard as evidence of strategic progress.

A single company-wide revenue total can conceal a drift towards the wrong customers. Separate target-fit relationships from exceptions when assessing acquisition, retention and service burden. Similarly, a rising number of created schedules can coexist with more manual correction. Include evidence about the outcome the strategy promises, not only activity that is easy to count.

Do not mistake a short measurement window for a complete answer. Setup may produce immediate work before later benefit. Reliability may look acceptable during quiet periods and fail under peak coordination. Some dissatisfied customers may remain because changing suppliers is difficult. Interpret measures with research and operating knowledge rather than treating every renewal or click as endorsement.

Set conditions for strategic review. Cedar might revisit the focus if suitable customers consistently require a capability it cannot economically provide, or if adoption succeeds but the promised coordination benefit remains absent. A major change in alternatives could also matter. The review should ask whether the diagnosis, approach, execution or measurement needs revision; those are different responses.

This avoids two unhelpful extremes: defending a strategy regardless of evidence and replacing it after every disappointment. Leadership needs enough continuity to develop capabilities, alongside a genuine route for disconfirming information to change commitments. State who makes that decision and how evidence reaches them. An assumption register without a decision owner cannot perform that function.

## Use AI to test coherence, then make the choice

AI can critique a proposed strategy if it receives the actual choices and constraints. Supply Cedar's target situation, evidence, current capabilities, resource limits, commercial model and exclusions. Ask it to identify contradictions among them. A generic prompt to “write a winning strategy for field-service software” lacks the information that makes the choice meaningful.

For a **coherence critique**, give the assistant the six-area table and a proposed sales commitment. Ask which capabilities the commitment requires, which assumptions it changes, and where it conflicts with the target customer. The output should point to specific statements. A vague warning to balance simplicity and flexibility adds little unless it identifies a decision.

For **competitive responses**, ask how a rival or the customer's existing arrangement could adapt. A rival might improve onboarding; a service business might standardise its own procedures. These are conditional scenarios, not predictions about named organisations. Assess whether the response is feasible and whether it removes the reason a suitable customer would choose Cedar.

For **unstated assumptions**, require the assistant to connect each desired outcome to the behaviour, data and operating capability it depends on. If the claim is reduced coordination effort, who must record changes consistently and what happens when someone does not? Compare the answer with actual research and technical knowledge. The assistant can expose a missing premise without establishing its truth.

For **alternative scenarios**, vary a few consequential conditions: customers need more setup help, a selected integration becomes unreliable, or the target firms rely more heavily on temporary staff. Ask which choices would remain useful and which would need revision. Do not let generated scenarios acquire invented probabilities or financial returns simply because a comparison table has empty cells.

Human judgement remains necessary because strategy involves commitments, priorities and incomplete evidence about a particular organisation. AI cannot determine those commitments merely by synthesising generic market information. It can make an argument easier to challenge; leadership must decide which risks and sacrifices to accept, with specialists assessing claims outside the team's competence.

Try a coherence exercise. Cedar receives the enterprise request for a bespoke approval hierarchy. Write the additional product, engineering, sales and support commitments it would create. Then choose among declining it, offering a bounded existing capability, investigating whether it serves the target workflow, or explicitly reconsidering the strategic focus. Explain the evidence that supports your choice and what would reverse it.

Finish with a sentence explaining the problem Cedar chooses to address, for whom, through which distinctive approach and under which limits. Then identify one consequential action the sentence supports and one attractive action it excludes. If the statement cannot help someone make those decisions, it still needs strategic work, however persuasive its ambition sounds.
