# Chapter 2: Value, Outcomes, and Constraints

## An improvement needs a comparison

Sending an appointment reminder and improving an appointment process are different achievements. A message can reach a homeowner's phone without helping the homeowner prepare for a visit. A reminder can also reveal that the agreed time is unsuitable, creating useful work for the office rather than removing work altogether. To assess the reminder, you need to explain whose situation should improve and how.

Consider the plumbing company's current arrangement. Its dispatcher calls homeowners in the afternoon to confirm the following day's visits. Cedar's product manager is considering a particular automatic routine: send one text at 4 p.m. the day before an appointment, to homeowners who have chosen texts, giving the agreed time and the company's telephone number. Whether those texts would replace some calls remains undecided. Their effects on attendance, workload, satisfaction and revenue are unknown.

The proposal sounds helpful. That is a reason to investigate, not a complete account of value. The owner wants fewer missed appointments. Homeowners want useful communication without unwanted messages. Technicians want fewer surprises in their working day. Cedar would incur messaging costs and responsibility for helping customers when the routine causes confusion or fails.

**Value is an improvement that matters to a specified person or group, assessed against an alternative and alongside costs and harms.** Each part matters. The improvement names a change. The beneficiary names who cares about it. The alternative describes what would otherwise happen. Costs and harms prevent a desirable change from being treated as an unqualified gain.

The relevant alternative here is the dispatcher's present calling arrangement, perhaps with modest improvements, rather than a world in which nobody receives a reminder. Comparing texts with no communication could exaggerate their expected contribution. Comparing them with perfectly executed calls could hide problems in the actual baseline. The product manager needs to understand what calls accomplish, which calls do not reach anyone, and what staff do with the replies they receive.

Alternatives can also include doing less, changing the timing of calls, letting homeowners check their appointments or addressing inaccurate contact information. Listing alternatives does not require treating every possibility as equally promising. It prevents the proposed feature from becoming the only imaginable route to an improvement.

This is why “customers will value reminders” is too imprecise to guide a decision. “Homeowners who prefer texts may find a written appointment time easier to check than information given during a call” names a possible benefit and a group. It also leaves a question that investigation can answer.

## Follow the change beyond delivery

An **output** is something produced or delivered by an activity. A reminder routine, a message sent and a help article explaining the routine are outputs. An **outcome** is a change in behaviour, circumstances or operating results. A homeowner rearranging their day, a dispatcher resolving a conflict earlier and a reduction in wasted journeys would be outcomes. Outcomes may be helpful, harmful, intended or unintended.

Outputs matter because they are the means through which change becomes possible. But counting messages sent cannot establish that appointments improved. A delivered message may go unread; a read message may be misunderstood; a correct message may reach somebody who cannot arrange access. Each possibility exposes another link between delivery and the desired result.

An **outcome chain** describes how a capability might change behaviour and work to produce a valued result. For the reminder proposal, one possible chain is:

> Cedar sends the agreed time and contact number → the homeowner notices a conflict and calls the office → the dispatcher rearranges the visit before the technician travels → the company avoids a wasted journey and the homeowner receives a workable appointment.

The capability is automatic reminder sending. The behaviour is the homeowner checking and calling. The operational change is the dispatcher handling the conflict before travel. The valued result is avoiding wasted work while arranging a usable appointment. Writing out those steps gives you places to investigate instead of a single optimistic statement.

The first step assumes that Cedar has the right appointment and contact information. The next assumes the homeowner reads the message soon enough and recognises what to do. The third assumes somebody answers the telephone and has a feasible alternative time. The final result depends on the revised arrangement reaching the people carrying out the work. A homeowner reminder alone does not solve the technician-information problem in Chapter 1.

This approach is related to a theory of change: an explanation of how an intervention is expected to produce results, with its assumptions and supporting evidence made explicit. HM Treasury's evaluation guidance emphasises examining those mechanisms, the affected groups and the conditions required for the intervention to work.[^c02-n01] You can use that discipline without turning a small product decision into a large evaluation programme.

An outcome chain is a proposed explanation, not proof. Even if missed appointments later decline, the decline alone would not show that reminders caused it. Other circumstances may have changed. For now, the useful question is where the proposed explanation is weak enough to change the design or justify further investigation.

## Several people, several kinds of value

One feature can affect several groups differently. A product manager needs to make those differences visible before choosing which improvements to pursue. The groups do not become interchangeable because a presentation calls them all “stakeholders”.

| Group | Possible improvement worth investigating | Possible tension |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homeowners | A convenient record of the agreed time and an easy route to contact the office | Repeated or poorly timed messages demand attention. |
| Plumbing-company owner | Fewer wasted visits and better use of paid working time | Additional communication or staffing may cost more. |
| Dispatcher | Earlier knowledge of conflicts | Calls may become concentrated immediately after reminders. |
| Technician | More dependable instructions and fewer wasted journeys | Late rearrangements may disrupt other commitments. |
| Cedar | A useful service customers have reason to keep using | Messaging, operation and support consume resources. |
| External messaging provider | Paid demand for a service it can deliver dependably | Support expectations and delivery responsibilities need agreement. |

These are candidate interests and consequences to examine, rather than findings from customer research. The homeowner and plumbing-company owner are both customers in everyday language, but they occupy different relationships. The owner buys Cedar; the homeowner buys repair work. Keeping their roles explicit avoids allowing the purchaser's preference to stand for every affected person's experience.

Customer value concerns whether people accomplish something that matters to them. Business value concerns the organisation's ability to sustain its purpose and resources. Employee value includes workable tasks and the burden of doing them. Partner value concerns organisations contributing to the arrangement. Operational value concerns the dependability and effort of the work itself. Societal value concerns effects beyond the immediate transaction, such as access to necessary services or avoidable travel.

Those categories overlap. Avoiding a wasted journey could help the technician, company and wider community. That does not permit you to count the same saving repeatedly and call the total several separate gains. Nor does it establish the environmental effect: fewer unnecessary journeys would be one relevant mechanism, but the actual travel change needs investigation.

A useful conversation names a priority and its limit. The company might prioritise fewer missed visits while refusing to increase unwanted communication. Cedar might seek lower support effort while preserving a way for homeowners to reach a person. These are choices about acceptable arrangements. They require judgement and discussion; the groups' different interests will not disappear inside one attractive score.

## Count what the change consumes

The visible cost of a reminder is the message charge. The full decision also includes the work of building and operating the routine, correcting contact information, explaining its behaviour and resolving complaints. A low cost per message can coexist with substantial effort elsewhere.

Time saved by one person may become time spent by another. If a text invites a homeowner to call with a problem, the dispatcher must handle that call. The call may be valuable because it prevents a wasted visit. Treating every incoming call as a cost to eliminate would miss its purpose. You need to understand the work before deciding which work should disappear.

Attention is another resource. A homeowner may receive reminders from several organisations, each convinced its message is useful. Cedar should investigate whether this particular message helps the recipient act, rather than assume that sending more information always helps. A second reminder could add convenience for one recipient and irritation for another.

Risk and complexity also belong in the assessment. An outdated appointment time could create confusion. Different communication preferences introduce cases that staff must understand. A routine that handles common cases cheaply may leave employees with a smaller number of unusually difficult exceptions. These are possible consequences to examine, not reasons to reject automation automatically.

Environmental effects matter where the proposal changes travel, energy use, materials or waste. State the mechanism and seek evidence proportionate to the decision. Do not attach a general environmental benefit to a software feature simply because one hoped-for outcome sounds efficient.

Finally, resources devoted to reminders cannot simultaneously fund every alternative. The same engineering capacity might improve contact records or help technicians receive changed instructions. The comparison includes what the organisation gives up by choosing this work now. Later economic reasoning will make that comparison more precise; already, naming the foregone alternative makes a claimed benefit more honest.

Costs and harms need not all be converted into money to be discussed seriously. Keep their units and affected people visible. Minutes of dispatcher time, unwanted messages and monetary charges describe different consequences. Combining them requires explicit judgements, not arithmetic that disguises the differences.

## Constraints define the feasible choice

A **constraint** is a condition limiting which choices are feasible or acceptable. Identifying one should lead to two further questions: where does it come from, and who can change it? Otherwise, a temporary organisational preference can acquire the authority of a physical limit.

Suppose the plumbing company's office closes at 5 p.m. Sending the proposed reminder at 4 p.m. leaves an hour for same-day calls. That fact does not prove that 4 p.m. is wrong. It makes the response process part of the design: how many calls can the dispatcher handle, what happens after closing, and can unresolved conflicts be dealt with before technicians leave? A message time cannot be chosen sensibly without considering the workforce available to respond.

Budget limits what Cedar can build and operate. Time limits when an investigation or change can help. Architecture, the way existing software is organised and connected, affects which information is available and which changes require substantial work. Capability limits what the organisation knows how to deliver and maintain. Calling a feature simple does not remove any of these conditions.

Regulation and contractual obligations may restrict communication and data use. The product manager should identify the jurisdictions, communication purpose and information involved, then involve appropriate legal or privacy expertise. A homeowner's choice to receive texts is part of this proposal; it is not a universal statement that every legal requirement has been satisfied.

Ethics concerns what the organisation considers acceptable even where an action is technically possible. Market conditions affect which alternatives customers have and what they expect. A company might be able to remove telephone support but decide that doing so excludes customers it intends to serve. Such a decision belongs in the product's design, rather than appearing later as a surprise to support staff.

Some constraints can be changed at a cost. The owner could extend office cover; Cedar could improve its information systems. Others may remain binding for the present decision. Record the distinction. “We cannot answer after 5 p.m. with current staffing” invites a meaningful choice about hours, timing or expectations. “Customers must adapt to the product” conceals that choice.

Constraints help define a feasible version of the proposal. They are part of the reasoning about value because an improvement that cannot be delivered or sustained is not an available option.

## Investigate the proposed value

AI can help expose missing groups and weak links before you commission research. Give it the actual proposal and baseline, then ask for candidate explanations:

> A dispatcher currently calls homeowners each afternoon about tomorrow's visits. We are considering one text at 4 p.m. to homeowners who have chosen texts, containing the agreed appointment time and office number. Replacing calls is undecided. Identify affected groups, possible benefits and costs, and two outcome chains. Mark every assumed behaviour and suggest a question that would test it. Do not invent customer findings or effect sizes.

Treat the response as material for investigation. If it says homeowners prefer texts, ask which evidence supports that claim. If it predicts that support calls disappear, trace what happens when a homeowner spots a conflict. If it includes a messaging partner, check the actual arrangement before assigning responsibilities. Generated interests remain hypotheses until grounded in evidence from the relevant people and operation.

Take a feature from your own work that colleagues consider obviously valuable. Identify at least four distinct potential beneficiaries or affected groups. For each, write the alternative they experience today, a possible improvement, a cost they may bear and an unintended outcome. Include somebody who uses the result without purchasing the product.

Choose one hoped-for improvement and trace the capability, behaviour, operational change and result. Mark what is known and what is assumed. Then name one constraint and one question whose answer could change whether you build the feature or how you offer it.

A useful answer exposes a decision. If every group benefits, no one bears a cost and every arrow happens automatically, revisit the people doing the work. If the exercise ends with a long list of uncertainties, select the uncertainty most likely to change your choice. The objective is a clearer next investigation, not a document that anticipates every possible event.

For Cedar, that next investigation might concern how confirmation calls currently resolve conflicts and whether the proposed text preserves that function. Product judgement begins with the same discipline elsewhere: value for whom, compared with what, at what cost, under what constraints?
