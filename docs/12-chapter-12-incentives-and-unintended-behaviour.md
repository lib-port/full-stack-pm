# Chapter 12: Incentives and Unintended Behaviour

## When the number improves and the work worsens

The technician's utilisation score rises, but the homeowner receives less help. After finishing a repair, Leon closes the job record and gives a hurried explanation before leaving for the next visit. The homeowner does not understand what follow-up has been arranged and telephones the office. Leon has protected the number his manager rewards while moving work onto somebody else.

Cedar has introduced a utilisation score intended to help service businesses improve efficiency. In this version of the score, recorded job time is divided by paid shift time. At the maintenance company employing Leon, the service manager, Lina, begins rewarding technicians with high scores. Work that counts towards the numerator now has an additional advantage over work that does not.

Technicians respond in two ways. They shorten unrecorded explanations and handovers so that they can begin the next recorded job sooner. Some also keep job timers running during travel that should be recorded separately. Customer communication worsens, and job-duration records become less dependable. A later dispatcher consulting those records cannot tell how long the repair itself took.

The arithmetic can remain correct while the meaning deteriorates. Six recorded job hours in an eight-hour shift produce a score of 75%. Reclassifying another hour as job time produces 87.5%, even if the amount of useful repair work is unchanged. The score measures what enters its calculation. It cannot independently establish that those entries describe better service.

An **incentive** is a consequence that makes an action more or less attractive to an actor. Money matters, but so do recognition, avoiding criticism, preserving autonomy, gaining access and reducing effort. Lina has changed the consequences of how technicians allocate time. The product's reporting function is now part of a management arrangement that shapes work.

**Gaming** means exploiting a rule to obtain its reward while undermining its purpose. A technician who selects easy jobs to protect a completion score may follow the stated rule while making the overall service worse. **Metric manipulation** changes what is recorded or classified so the indicator improves without the intended underlying improvement. Leaving a timer running across unrelated activity is an example. The two can overlap, but they require different investigations.

The chain is specific: Cedar makes a number visible; the manager attaches recognition to it; technicians redirect effort and reporting; customers receive less explanation; the office handles avoidable questions; future planning uses less reliable data. The harmful effects follow through the people and information involved. Merely saying “the metric has unintended consequences” would hide the mechanism needed to change it.

Understanding that mechanism does not excuse falsifying records. It also does not justify treating every technician as dishonest. The product manager needs to examine the pressure created by the rule, the work it omits and the practical options employees have. A better system should make useful work possible to perform and reasonable to report accurately.

Lina also faces incentives. If her own manager praises only the utilisation average, she has a reason to emphasise that average in meetings and give less attention to inconvenient service reports. Cedar's sales staff may find a simple efficiency number easy to demonstrate. Following the chain therefore means examining more than the person entering the data. The rule travels through purchasing, reporting and supervision, acquiring consequences that its designer may not have considered. A technician's response can be the most visible part of an arrangement reinforced elsewhere.

## Who knows what, and who bears the consequence?

Lina wants dependable repairs, sensible use of staff time and satisfied customers. Leon must decide how to spend minutes across travel, repair, explanation, documentation and helping colleagues. Lina cannot observe every action, and the utilisation score captures only part of what she values.

This is a **principal–agent problem**: one party relies on another to act on its behalf, but their interests may differ and the first party cannot fully observe or assess the relevant actions. Lina acts for the employer; Leon carries out the work. Either person can be conscientious while the arrangement creates a conflict between the measured reward and the broader objective.

**Information asymmetry** means that the parties have different relevant information. Leon knows more about the particular visit than Lina can infer from its recorded duration. Lina may know more about upcoming demand and financial constraints. The point is not that one person knows everything. It is that information affecting the decision is unevenly available.[^c12-n01]

Making more data visible can help, but it does not remove the choice of what matters. Tracking every minute may consume staff attention, discourage legitimate discretion and create further categories to manipulate. A manager still needs to distinguish a difficult job from a poor performance and an honest correction from a misleading entry.

Economic work on tasks with several dimensions helps explain the problem: rewarding a readily measured activity can direct attention away from valuable duties that are harder to measure.[^c12-n02] In Leon's case, the reporting design makes job time prominent while leaving the quality of a handover less visible. The product manager should ask which duties lose attention when the rewarded number becomes more important.

**Moral hazard** describes an incentive problem in which actions that are difficult for another party to observe or enforce can change when the actor does not bear their full consequences.[^c12-n03] Suppose a repair marketplace guarantees to pay for correcting defective work but does not inspect providers' checks. A provider could spend less effort checking the work because the platform absorbs the correction cost. That is a possible response to the arrangement, not proof that any provider behaves that way.

Moral hazard is not a synonym for a bad person, nor does it require insurance. The useful question concerns hidden action and the distribution of its consequences. Customers, employers, platforms and product teams can all face such incentives. A team rewarded only for releasing on time may defer operational work whose cost will be borne by support colleagues later.

Ask who can see the action, who receives the reward and who handles the resulting problem. When those are different people, the product design needs a way to connect their information and responsibilities.

## Who joins, who pays and who benefits?

Some incentive problems arise before work begins. Consider a marketplace on which households choose repair providers. The platform presents every provider as equally qualified but has no reliable way to distinguish careful work from poor work. Providers know more about their own capability than prospective customers do.

If buyers cannot recognise better quality, they may be unwilling to pay enough to attract providers who incur the cost of delivering it. Those providers may leave, while providers offering lower quality remain willing to accept the available price. The pool changes, making the original uncertainty worse. This is **adverse selection**: participation or trading choices under unequal information produce a less favourable mix.[^c12-n01]

The distinction from moral hazard is useful. Adverse selection concerns the information and characteristics affecting who enters a transaction. Moral hazard concerns actions whose incentives change within an arrangement. In the marketplace, misleading membership signals could attract an unsuitable provider mix; an unconditional correction guarantee could affect how a participating provider performs work. Both can exist together, but the remedies need not be the same.

Verified qualifications, examples of work, meaningful guarantees or evidence from completed jobs may help customers distinguish providers. Each mechanism has costs and limitations. A badge that merely records payment of a membership fee cannot establish competence. A review system needs consideration of who can leave reviews, what the reviewers actually observed and whether providers can influence what is displayed.

The marketplace also affects people outside its transactions. Suppose its scheduling rules encourage several contractors to arrive on the same residential street at once. Their vehicles obstruct access for neighbours who neither booked a repair nor agreed to bear that inconvenience. The obstruction is an **externality**: an effect on others that the participants' own costs or rewards do not fully account for.[^c12-n04] An externality can also be beneficial; the issue is the gap between private incentives and wider consequences.

**Free-riding** arises when someone benefits from a shared contribution while leaving others to provide it.[^c12-n05] In a provider community, careful members might spend time maintaining a shared fault guide that all members can use. A provider can benefit without contributing. If too few people find contributing worthwhile, the guide becomes outdated even though everyone would benefit from an accurate version.

That possibility does not prove that every member should contribute identically. Some may have useful expertise but little time; others may fund the service. The design question is how the shared benefit will be sustained. Membership fees, paid editorial work, recognition or reciprocal contributions produce different arrangements. The product manager needs to consider who supplies the underlying work, rather than assume participation automatically maintains a common resource.

These mechanisms also suggest different evidence. To investigate adverse selection, examine who declines to join and why, alongside the quality signals buyers can inspect. To investigate moral hazard, examine what participating providers can change after acceptance and who bears the resulting cost. For free-riding, investigate whether contributions sustain the shared resource and whether contributors consider the arrangement workable. One complaint about a provider cannot distinguish all three. Naming the mechanism should sharpen the enquiry, not merely attach an academic term to dissatisfaction.

## Design the rule around the work

Return to Cedar's score. Adding a second number for customer satisfaction may sound like an easy repair. Yet technicians could then concentrate on customers most likely to give favourable feedback, and surveys could burden homeowners. Every new rule creates another set of possible responses. More measurement does not automatically produce better incentives.

Start by naming the desired behaviour in ordinary language: complete appropriate repairs, explain what happens next, record useful information and help the company organise dependable service. Then examine which of those activities employees control, which depend on their circumstances and which can reasonably be assessed. A technician cannot make a difficult journey short simply because travel time harms a score.

For this company, Lina could stop using the utilisation figure as an individual reward trigger while keeping it as a prompt for investigating scheduling patterns. She could review explanations and handovers as part of ordinary supervision, give staff a way to correct inaccurate entries and discuss why certain jobs require more time. Those are proposals to test, not a guaranteed solution to the case.

Cedar's product manager has related choices. The interface can explain what the calculation includes, show relevant context and make it harder to mistake one ratio for overall performance. Product guidance can describe appropriate interpretations and limitations. Cedar can investigate how managers actually use the report rather than treating the existence of a disclaimer as sufficient control.

This is the practical intuition behind **mechanism design**: begin with the result sought, then consider rules under which people's available actions and interests can help produce that result. A mechanism includes what information people supply, how decisions are made and which consequences follow. The intuition is useful here without claiming that an optimal mathematical mechanism has been found.

Look for ways to make accurate reporting and useful work compatible. If admitting a delay always triggers punishment, hiding the delay becomes more attractive. If staff can report a problem early and receive help, disclosure may become more useful to them. Whether either response occurs depends on credibility, past experience and daily practice; changing a label in the interface cannot establish trust.

Controls also need proportionality. Surveillance can carry privacy and employment implications, so involve the relevant specialists and worker representatives when appropriate. Managers and technicians can explain whether a proposed check is feasible. The product manager contributes by clarifying the intended behaviour, the likely trade-offs and the information needed to assess the arrangement.

Finally, review the rule after adoption. People learn its details and adapt over time. A measure that was initially informative can become less informative once rewards depend on it. The organisation needs a way to notice deteriorating data or service and change the arrangement without assuming that every improvement in the score represents progress.

Before changing an incentive, explain what will happen to existing rewards and disputed records. Otherwise, staff may reasonably fear that the rules will change after they have acted on them. Predictability and a way to challenge mistakes can matter as much as the chosen formula. The product manager should agree those responsibilities with the people who operate the arrangement.

## Try to break the proposal before adopting it

AI can help generate ways a rule might be exploited. This is useful for finding questions and designing checks, especially when the rule's authors have concentrated on its intended use. It does not establish how actual employees or customers will respond.

For Cedar's report, a useful task is:

> A manager rewards high recorded job time as a share of paid shift time. Technicians also travel, explain repairs, record notes and help colleagues. Generate plausible ways someone could improve the score without improving service. For each, identify the action, the information the manager cannot see, who benefits, who bears the cost and what evidence would distinguish the behaviour from a legitimate explanation. Include responses that follow the rule honestly as well as misleading reporting.

Inspect the response for feasibility. A proposed exploit may require permissions technicians do not have or assume a reward they do not receive. Ask an operational colleague to check the workflow. If the assistant predicts that every technician will falsify time, reject the unsupported generalisation. A generated possibility justifies investigating whether the conditions exist; it is not grounds for accusing an employee.

Then ask for alternative rules and their own failure modes. A team reward might support cooperation, but it could also weaken the connection between individual effort and recognition. A review of exceptions might improve context, but it could become a bottleneck or discourage honest reporting. Useful analysis includes the cost of the remedy and what would reveal that it is failing.

Try the same exercise with a rule in your product: a cancellation fee, completion badge, referral reward or ranking. Write down the intended action and the easiest alternative action that obtains the reward. Trace the consequences for the person acting, another participant and somebody outside the immediate transaction. State whether you are describing known behaviour or a hypothesis.

Choose one check that could change the proposal. You might inspect permission settings, talk through a realistic case with participants, review existing records or run a limited trial with appropriate oversight. Keep normal variation and deliberate manipulation separate: a low score may reflect difficult work, and an unusual entry may be a correction.

The habitual question is straightforward: how could a person pursuing a reasonable interest respond to this rule in a way we did not intend? Following that question through actions, information and consequences helps product managers build arrangements that support the work they actually want done.
