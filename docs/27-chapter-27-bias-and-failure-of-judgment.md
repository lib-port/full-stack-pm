# Chapter 27: Bias and Failure of Judgement

## Change the question before collecting more support

Cedar's leadership favours AI-assisted scheduling. A service-business owner says automation sounds useful, and the comment enters the presentation as support. A dispatcher says that existing promises must remain under her control, and the comment is described as a training concern. Evidence is being collected, but different observations are being asked to pass different tests.

The problem is not that leadership has a preference. Product decisions involve preferences and commitments. The problem is that a preferred answer can shape what counts as evidence before the investigation has had a fair chance to challenge it.

**Confirmation bias** involves seeking, interpreting or remembering information in ways that support an existing belief. **Motivated reasoning** describes reasoning influenced by a desired conclusion. In Cedar's discussion, an attractive strategic story can make supportive comments seem decisive and objections seem temporary. These terms describe possible mechanisms; they do not establish what a particular colleague secretly intends.

Ask what the same evidence would mean if it concerned a less favoured proposal. Would one enthusiastic comment demonstrate demand? Would a control concern be treated as a material requirement? Unequal scrutiny is something the team can inspect without accusing individuals of dishonesty.

Change the investigation from “find support for AI scheduling” to a decision with alternatives. Cedar needs to determine whether a scheduling capability can reduce coordination effort while respecting appointment promises and allowing dispatchers to understand and correct suggestions. Possible responses include assisted suggestions, improvements to the existing manual process and further investigation before either commitment.

Agree **precommitted criteria** before the next evidence arrives. State which observations would support a limited trial, which would require redesign and which would stop the proposal. The criteria might concern essential control, usable information and acceptable recovery from a poor suggestion. They need enough specificity that colleagues cannot reinterpret every result as success.

Make the evidence review equally concrete. Keep the owner’s enthusiasm as evidence of a stated interest, with the situation and question that elicited it. Keep the dispatcher’s concern as evidence of a constraint to investigate. Neither observation, on its own, answers whether the proposed system can make acceptable scheduling suggestions in practice. The next inquiry should examine that capability with representative work, including the cases most likely to challenge it.

Before reviewing those cases, ask the team to write what a disappointing result would look like. If every possible result can be described as encouraging, the investigation cannot change the decision. The remedy may be to narrow the question until a result can distinguish options, rather than gathering more of the same comments. Give someone responsibility for retaining contradictory observations in the final account so that synthesis does not quietly become advocacy.

Precommitment is not a promise to ignore new information. If the decision or a critical assumption changes, revise the criteria openly and record the reason. Quietly changing them after a disappointing result defeats their purpose.

In experiments on social judgement, Lord, Lepper and Preston found that asking participants to consider opposite possibilities corrected the studied biases more effectively than a general instruction to be fair and unbiased.[^c27-n01] That finding does not guarantee better product decisions. It suggests a useful distinction: a concrete thinking task can change the process where an exhortation alone leaves it untouched.

For Cedar, the concrete task is to seek evidence that the proposed capability would not solve the dispatcher’s problem, and evidence that would challenge the manual alternative too. A fair investigation exposes each option to meaningful possible failure.

## Protect the first estimate and look for missing cases

The first information presented can shape later judgement. **Anchoring** occurs when an initial value or framing exerts undue influence on subsequent estimates. If a senior leader announces an expected saving before colleagues form their own views, discussion may revolve around adjusting that number rather than deriving an estimate from the work.

Ask people with relevant perspectives to make initial assessments independently. The dispatcher estimates which activities could change; the engineer identifies technical uncertainty; the researcher describes what the available observations support. Each records assumptions and a range where appropriate before seeing the leader's preferred number.

Independence here means independence of the initial assessment, not permanent isolation. Compare the reasoning afterwards. Large differences may reveal different task definitions or information rather than incompetence. Combining identical guesses copied from one source provides less information than understanding why informed estimates differ.

**Availability** makes information that comes readily to mind influential in judgement. A vivid complaint from yesterday can dominate a quieter pattern across months. The complaint may reveal a severe problem worth addressing even if rare; its memorability alone does not establish frequency. Separate the seriousness of an event from the estimate of how often it occurs.

For Cedar, preserve the dispatcher’s detailed concern, then investigate where and how often that constraint arises. Do not counter one vivid story with a larger pile of vague enthusiasm. Compare observations that answer the same question and retain important exceptions even when they are uncommon.

**Survivorship bias** appears when conclusions rely on the people or attempts that remain visible while excluding those that disappeared. A successful pilot participant can explain what worked for them. They cannot represent companies that declined, abandoned setup or never met the entry requirements.

Review the path into the evidence. Which companies received an invitation? Which accepted? Which completed the trial? Which stopped responding? Cedar's analysis should retain these groups and the limits of what is known about them. An empty cell is an uncertainty to investigate, not permission to assume that absent companies would behave like the remaining participants.

This matters in hiring tools, educational services and marketplaces too. Learning only from successful applicants, graduates or active sellers can conceal barriers encountered by people who never reached the visible outcome. The general correction is to examine how the evidence became observable.

Together, these practices improve the information entering the decision: delay a common anchor, distinguish vividness from prevalence and look beyond survivors. They do not require every person to recognise every bias in the moment. They change what the group is able to see.

## Make forecasts and disagreement usable

**Overconfidence** arises when certainty exceeds what the evidence justifies. It can appear as an unnecessarily narrow range, a prediction treated as inevitable or a refusal to consider an important failure mode. The remedy is not ritual pessimism. It is to make the grounds and limits of the estimate explicit.

Cedar's scheduling plan may assume that appointment records are complete, dispatcher approval is quick and recommendations require little explanation. Separate these assumptions. A demonstration using tidy records provides limited evidence about messy operational data; a positive reaction provides limited evidence about continued use during a difficult working day.

The **planning fallacy** concerns underestimating the time or resources a task will require while concentrating on how the intended plan should unfold. Cedar's team can imagine the direct path from prototype to launch and treat data repair, permission design and support preparation as exceptional interruptions. If comparable work repeatedly included those activities, excluding them from the estimate needs justification.

Buehler, Griffin and Ross's studies examined optimistic estimates of personal task-completion times and the attention given to plans versus past experience. Their discussion also distinguishes reducing optimistic bias from improving absolute accuracy.[^c27-n02] Simply making every estimate later is not the same as making estimates better.

Use relevant completed work as a comparison, including the inconvenient parts. Ask what makes this proposal easier or harder, what dependencies remain unresolved and what evidence supports the difference. Preserve an explicit forecast so the team can later check its reasoning, rather than remembering whichever version matches the outcome.

Group discussion adds another risk. **Groupthink** describes a process in which pressure for agreement suppresses critical examination. Silence after an enthusiastic leadership presentation may mean agreement, uncertainty, lack of preparation or reluctance to challenge authority. It is weak evidence of consensus.

Create time for written concerns before the meeting and invite people with operational knowledge to explain them. A person describing a failure mode should not have to defeat the whole strategy to have that concern addressed. Ask the decision-maker to state which concerns change the proposal, which remain unresolved and why any residual risk is accepted.

Dissent needs a response, not a ceremonial speaking slot. If a dispatcher says the proposed assistant may silently change a promise, Cedar can investigate permission design and make that behaviour an explicit test condition. Dismissing the concern as resistance to innovation teaches the group which evidence is unwelcome.

Protect the distinction between challenging a claim and challenging a person's standing. A researcher can question whether comments demonstrate willingness to adopt without claiming authority over the commercial strategy. An engineer can identify a dependency without deciding which customer outcome matters most. Clear boundaries let colleagues contribute relevant criticism without turning every disagreement into a contest over who controls the whole decision.

The person with decision authority still decides. A process that hears disagreement does not require unanimity or endless debate. It requires a record of the important arguments, the basis for commitment and what would justify reopening the choice. That record makes confident action compatible with continuing uncertainty.

## Separate future value from past commitment

After investing in a scheduling prototype, Cedar may face evidence that a required capability will cost more than expected. The question becomes whether further investment is worthwhile compared with the available alternatives. Money already spent cannot be recovered by making the next decision feel consistent.

The **sunk-cost effect** occurs when irrecoverable past expenditure receives undue weight in a forward-looking choice. “We have already invested too much to stop” does not explain why the next increment of work will create value. Ask what the team would choose now if the current assets and knowledge were available without a need to defend the earlier decision.

Past work can still matter. A usable component may reduce future cost; an existing commitment can create real obligations; stopping can require transition work. These are present or future consequences, not reasons to count the same past expenditure again. Separate them in the comparison so that stopping and continuing receive equally honest treatment.

Make staged commitments where the decision permits. Cedar can investigate a critical scheduling assumption before authorising a larger build. State what that stage should reveal and what happens afterwards. A “small trial” without a decision boundary can become a sequence of extensions that never faces the original question.

Once results arrive, **hindsight bias** can make the outcome seem more foreseeable than it was. After a difficult rollout, colleagues may remember themselves as having predicted every problem. After a successful one, they may remember broad confidence where the earlier discussion contained substantial doubt.

Keep the original reasoning, forecasts and dissent. Review what information was available at the time, which uncertainties were recognised and which signals were missed. Distinguish a foreseeable problem ignored in the decision from a development the team had little reasonable basis to anticipate.

Also distinguish process quality from outcome luck. A careful choice can produce a result it explicitly allowed for but hoped to avoid. A weakly supported choice can succeed. If the review rewards only favourable outcomes, people have reason to disguise uncertainty and rewrite history rather than improve their judgement.

A useful review asks what should change in the next decision process. Perhaps estimates need an independent first pass, pilot selection needs wider coverage or unresolved risks need a named owner. “Be more careful” leaves the mechanism unchanged. Choose a concrete adjustment that colleagues can observe in the next comparable decision.

Do not use bias labels as a weapon against whoever disagrees. A person can rationally favour a different option because they value outcomes differently or have different authority over risk. Clarify the factual claim, assumption, forecast or preference in dispute before deciding that a reasoning failure explains it.

## Use AI to challenge the process as well as the proposal

AI can generate counterarguments, alternative explanations and questions that a team has overlooked. It can also produce an elaborate defence of the conclusion embedded in the prompt. Asking “explain why AI scheduling is the right strategic move” makes a different task from asking whether the available evidence distinguishes scheduling assistance from other ways to reduce coordination work.

Give the assistant a balanced evidence packet, the actual decision, alternatives and explicit assumptions. Ask it to produce the strongest case for each option, the evidence each case depends on and observations that would weaken it. Require a distinction between supplied facts, interpretations and hypothetical objections.

Then ask for a critique of the decision process. Did the leader's forecast precede independent estimates? Are trial dropouts missing? Would the same evidence standard be applied to a competing proposal? Has the planned review date arrived without a decision? These questions connect the named biases to arrangements the organisation can change.

Check generated counterarguments with people and sources. An assistant's claim that dispatchers will reject automation is no more empirical evidence than its claim that they will welcome it. Use the claim to design an inquiry. Do not replace selective optimism with selective pessimism and call the result balanced.

Different prompts or several assistants may still repeat the same assumptions. Treat agreement as a property of the generated material until supported by independent evidence. Protect confidential plans and participant information according to the organisation's approved data practices when preparing the evidence packet.

For practice, choose a decision with an obvious preferred answer. Identify the point at which that preference could influence information collection, estimation, discussion or review. Select two concrete process changes before collecting more evidence. One might be independent estimates before the meeting; another might be a requirement to examine abandoned trials alongside successful ones.

Write what would count as disconfirming evidence and who will ensure it receives a response. Set a review point and retain the current assumptions. The exercise succeeds when the next decision exposes a material disagreement or uncertainty that the previous process would have hidden, then deals with it explicitly.

Some changes require facilitation, research expertise or statistical support; others require a leader to alter how authority is exercised. No individual product manager can make an organisation impartial by privately learning a list of biases. They can help build a process in which weak claims encounter better questions before commitment, and outcomes improve the next round of judgement.
